Abstract 26 

Our understanding of the earliest genomic processes and evolutionary trajectories that 27 distinguish sporadic from hereditary human cancers remains limited, impeding early diagnosis 28 and risk stratification. Here, we performed whole-genome sequencing of 319 single crypts 29 dissected from normal colorectal mucosa, premalignant polyps and malignant colorectal cancers 30 (CRCs) from five sporadic patients and three familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) patients. 31 We found 66% of premalignant crypts from sporadic polyps did not harbor APC mutations, but 32 frequently accumulated non-canonical but positively selected driver mutations including KMT2C, 33 

CACNA1A and FBLN2 . In contrast, most premalignant crypts (71%) in FAP indeed acquired 34 one or more somatic APC mutations or 5q loss of heterogeneity (LOH). Sporadic polyps were 35 dominated by clock-like mutational processes at single-crypt level. In contrast, FAP crypts 36 evolved more diverse genomics processes, including reactive oxygen species-associated 37 damages, APOBEC-associated mutagenesis and pks + Escherichia coli-associated mutagenesis. 38 Premalignant crypts in FAP also exhibited a 2-fold higher burden of LINE-1 retrotranspositions 39 than sporadic ones, despite arising at a substantially younger age. Phylogenetic timing 40 estimations showed that the first somatic APC mutation in FAP often occurs prenatally. 41 Phylogeographic analysis further revealed spatial lineage segregation in early polyclonal polyps, 42 followed by Big-Bang lineage intermixing in later monoclonal ones. Together, these data 43 demonstrate that early colorectal tumorigenesis does not obey a universal APC -first model, 44 where sporadic and hereditary precancers follow distinct evolutionary trajectories. Our study 45 also highlights that polyclonal-to-monoclonal transition in premalignant stages represents the 46 Big-Bang growth of colorectal tumors, offering crucial insight into early diagnosis and risk 47 stratification. 48

Introduction 51 

Understanding the earliest evolutionary steps of tumorigenesis is crucial for cancer risk 52 stratification and prevention 1-6 . Unlike malignant cancers, which are evolutionarily late in 53 tumorigenesis 7,8 , premalignant lesions provide natural “fossils” recording early evolutionary 54 processes 5,9,10 . Colorectal cancers (CRCs) develop through the canonical adenoma-carcinoma 55 sequence, occurring in both sporadic or hereditary forms 11 . Sporadic CRCs accounts for more 56 than 90% of cases and is generally thought to emerge through the gradual accumulation of 57 somatic alterations in canonical CRC driver genes (such as APC , KRAS , TP53 ), leading to the 58 malignant transformation from premalignant adenoma or polyps 12,13 . Hereditary CRC develops 59 in a background of germline predisposition. Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), usually 60 caused by a germline APC mutation, accounts for ~1% of all CRCs characterized by the early 61 appearance of hundreds to thousands of adenomatous polyps throughout the colorectum 14,15 .62 Without prophylactic intervention, FAP patients are at an extremely high-risk of developing CRC. 63 Therefore, examining sporadic and FAP premalignant polyps provide critical insights into the 64 tempo and mode of tumor initiation and evolution 16-19 .65 Through lineage tracing combined with genomic analysis, we and others have demonstrated 66 that early intestinal lesions are commonly of polyclonal origins in both mouse models 19-22 and 67 human premalignant polyps 18,19 . These findings challenge the classical view that a colorectal 68 neoplasia is initiated by a single progenitor cell 23 . Instead, early lesions may arise through 69 cooperation among multiple progenitor clones before later monoclonal progression 18-20,24 .70 However, a systematic characterization of genomic landscape and evolutionary dynamics that 71 distinguish sporadic from hereditary precancers are still unclear. This is crucial for refining risk 72 stratification and developing interception strategies tailored to distinct evolutionary trajectories. 73 The normal intestinal epithelium and neoplasia are both organized into glandular units known 74 as crypts, or glands. Each human crypt contains several thousands of cells 25 and is maintained 75 by a small number of stem cells in normal conditions 26 . This glandular spatial architecture in the 76 intestine enables the sequencing of individual crypts as naturally occurring clones, resolving 77 somatic clonal relationships at near single-clone resolution. Although single-crypt sequencing 78 has been widely used to study the normal colorectal epithelium 27,28 , inflammatory bowel disease 79 (IBD) 29 and CRC 30-32 , its use in premalignant polyps remains limited. Consequently, the genomic 80 processes and evolutionary dynamics operating in premalignant polyps at single-clone 81 resolution are still poorly understood. 82 To fill this gap, we generated a large-scale, multi-regional single-crypt whole-genome 83 sequencing (WGS) datasets to define the genomic trajectories of human sporadic and hereditary 84 colorectal polyps. We profiled a total of 46 tissues (or lesions) spanning normal mucosa, 85 premalignant polyps and synchronous tumors from five sporadic patients and three FAP patients. 86 A validation cohort of 107 colorectal polyps with tissue-level bulk whole-exome sequencing 87 (WES) was also included 19 . Through a comprehensive analysis of somatic mutations, copy-88 number alterations, LINE-1 retrotransposition and phylogenetic trees, we identified distinct 89 genomic and evolutionary processes operating in sporadic and FAP tissues. We further show 90 that the polyclonal-to-monoclonal transition is accompanied by a spatial shift from lineage 91 segregation to lineage intermixing, which is consistent with the onset of Big-Bang tumor growth. 92

Results 93 

Single-crypt WGS of synchronous colorectal polyps and cancers 94 

We collected freshly resected specimens from five sporadic patients with synchronous CRC and 95 polyps and three FAP patients. Two of the three FAP patients had developed CRCs by the time 96 of surgery, at ages 35 (FAP01) and 24 (FAP04) ( Supplementary Table 1 ). None of the sporadic 97 patients had a history of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). In total, 46 tissue samples were 98 collected, spanning from normal mucosa (n = 16) and premalignant polyps (n = 26) to 99 synchronous malignant adenocarcinomas (n = 4) (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1,100 

Supplementary Tables 1-2 ). 101 Single crypts were isolated following established protocols 19,30 , with microscopic inspection used 102 to verify glandular morphology ( Fig. 1a-c ). Multiple spatial regions (~5 per tissue) were dissected 103 from each tissue. Individual crypts were then isolated (~8 crypts per tissue ; Fig. 1c ) and whole-104 genome sequenced at a mean depth of ~24X using a low-input DNA library preparation 105 protocol 33 (Supplementary Table 3). Somatic single nucleotide variants (SSNVs), short 106 insertions/deletions (indels), and somatic copy number alterations (SCNAs) were identified 107 using in-house bioinformatic pipelines as our published studies 19,34,35 (Methods ). 108 Among the 248 polypous crypts examined, 138 (55.6%) showed an irregular phenotype 109 characterized by abnormal morphology and increased length ( Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 2, 110 

Methods ). The remaining 38.7% retained a relatively regular morphology, despite harboring 111 significantly more SSNVs than normal mucosal crypts ( Fig. 1e ). Notably, irregular crypts 112 generally harbored significantly more SSNVs than morphologically regular ones ( Fig. 1e ), 113 indicating that genetic and morphological changes evolve progressively during precancerous 114 growth ( Supplementary Fig. 2g ). Polypous crypts from both sporadic and FAP patients showed 115 low levels of SCNAs (mean fraction of genome altered or FGA: 1.32%), though irregular crypts 116 in FAP showed relatively higher SCNAs burdens ( Fig. 1f ). Consistent with this, SSNV burden in 117 single crypts increased from normal tissues to polyp and then to tumors, whereas FGA remained 118 low in most premalignant crypts ( Fig. 1g-h, Supplementary Fig. 3 ). 119 These findings indicate that morphologically regular crypts in premalignant polyps have already 120 accumulated substantial somatic mutations, as 2-3 folds of adjacent normal crypts (~2.1-fold in 121 sporadic and ~2.6-fold in FAP). Therefore, continuous mutation accumulation precedes 122 morphological changes characterizes the earliest stage of CRC initiation. 123 

Distinct genomic signatures in sporadic and FAP polyps 124 

Somatic mutational signatures reflect the activity of endogenous cellular processes, exposure 125 to exogenous environmental or lifestyle mutagens 36 . To evaluate the mutational activity at the 126 single-crypt level, we extracted the single base substitutions (SBS) signatures using a127 hierarchical Dirichlet process (HDP) 37 , and estimated the mutational processes of each crypt 128 using sigfit 38 (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Table 3 ). We identified eight signatures showing strong 129 similarity to reference signatures in the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC) 130 database, SBS1 and SBS5 as clock-like signatures, SBS2 caused by catalytic polypeptide-like 131 (APOBEC) DNA-editing enzymes 39 , SBS3 caused by homologous repair deficiency, SBS18 132 caused by DNA damage by reactive oxygen species (ROS) 40 , SBS88 caused by 133 

pks + Escherichia coli (E.coli) 41 , SBS17b and SBS52 with unknown cause ( Fig. 1i ). 134 Somatic mutations in both normal and polypous crypts were dominated by clock-like signatures 135 (SBS1 and SBS5) ( Fig. 1j ). This pattern was more pronounced in sporadic polyps than in FAP 136 polyps, consistent with the older ages (mean age 64 of sporadic vs. 32 years old of FAP) and 137

Distinct CRC drivers underlie sporadic and hereditary polyp initiation 152 

Having observed marked differences in genomic processes, we next asked whether sporadic 153 and hereditary polyps also differ in their driver mutation landscapes at the single-crypt level ( Fig. 154 

2, Supplementary Fig. 4 ). Somatic APC mutations were rare in normal mucosal crypts (10% 155 or 1 out of 10 in FAP, 6% or 1 out of 17 in sporadic patients), consistent with previous 156 study 18,27,29,47 . Unexpectedly, only 34% of premalignant crypts from sporadic polyps harbored 157 somatic APC mutations ( Fig. 2a-b, Supplementary Fig. 4a ). In contrast, 71% of premalignant 158 crypts from FAP indeed carried one or more somatic APC mutations or 5q LOH, in addition to 159 the germline APC mutation ( Fig. 2a-b, Supplementary Fig. 4b ). We then examined the three 160 canonical CRC driver genes ( APC , KRAS and TP53 , hereafter abbreviated as AKP) and defined 161 AKP and non-AKP crypts as those with or without AKP somatic alterations, respectively ( Fig. 162 

2b) .163 Strikingly, nearly half (46% or 38 out of 82) of the sporadic premalignant crypts were non- AKP, 164 whereas this proportion was much lower in FAP (26%, 38 out of 146, Fig. 2b ). Importantly, non-165 AKP crypts in sporadic polyps often showed more irregular morphology than those in FAP 166 polyps ( Fig. 2c-d ), indicating that these non-AKP crypts were bona fide dysplastic crypts . In 167 sporadic polyps, putative driver genes including KMT2C , CACNA1A , FAT2 , CTNNB1 , FBLN2, 168 

etc were more frequently mutated in non-AKP crypts than in AKP ones ( Fig. 2e ). However, few 169 genes were found in FAP polyps for this comparison ( Fig. 2f ). In fact, these driver genes like 170 

KMT2C , CACNA1A and FBLN2 also showed stronger selection in sporadic polyps compared to 171 normal mucosal crypts ( Fig. 2g, Supplementary Fig. 4c-d ), suggesting they confer a fitness 172 advantage during the earliest stages of initiation. These findings indicate that early sporadic 173 adenomas are often initiated through non-AKP CRC driver mutations, whereas adenomas in 174 FAP patients tend to follow the APC double-hit trajectory because of the germline heterozygous 175 

APC background. 176 We then examined whether somatic drivers in sporadic and FAP polyps were associated 177 genomic evolutionary trajectories by subgrouping premalignant crypts into non-AKP, APC -178 mutant, KRAS -mutant or TP53 -mutant ones ( Fig. 2h-i, Supplementary Fig. 5 ). Interestingly, 179 we found that non-AKP polypous crypts harbored a comparable number of somatic mutations 180 with APC -mutant crypts but fewer than KRAS -mutant or TP53 -mutant crypts in sporadic patients 181 (Fig. 2h ). This pattern was also reflected in clock-like mutations (SBS1 and SBS5) ( Fig. 2i, 182 

Supplementary Fig. 5b ). These data suggest that, in sporadic tissues, non-AKP and APC -183 mutant clones may expand in parallel during early lesion development. In FAP, by contrast, 184

Somatic APC mutations often arise prenatally in FAP patients 188 

To determine when key driver mutations arise, we estimated the timing of somatic events along 189 the phylogenetic trees using a molecular clock model, as in a previous study 48 . We reconstructed 190 single-crypt WGS phylogenies at the patient level, including all crypts from different polyps of 191 the same patient. Somatic mutations were assigned to corresponding branches and timing of 192 the events was inferred as the age from fertilization, while accounting for patient-specific and 193 clade-specific mutation rates and the elevated mutation rate during embryonic development 49 

194 (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 4, Methods ). Since somatic mutations accumulate 195 approximately linearly with age along each branch, mutations located on upper branches of a 196 phylogenetic tree indicate their earlier acquisition ( Methods ). 197 Strikingly, two out of three FAP patients (FAP04 and FAP01) acquired the first somatic APC 198 mutation within the first year of life from fertilization. Specifically, FAP04 acquired the 199 

APC R1381Ffs*9 mutation at 0.6 years (7.2 months) old from zygote (95% confidence interval or CI: 200 0.45–0.87) ( Fig. 3a ), and FAP01 acquired the APC R226X mutation at 0.12 years (1.4 months) old 201 from fertilization (95% CI: 0.08–0.16) ( Fig. 3b ). In FAP01, the somatic APC R1096X mutation and 202 two APC 5qLOH events occurred at about 15.57 (95% CI: 15.12–16.09), 20.24 (95% CI: 19.90– 203 20.58), and 18.94 (95% CI: 18.41–19.68) years old, respectively ( Fig. 3b ). In FAP03, two 204 somatic APC mutations occurred at about 10.46 (95%: 8.77–14.57) and 13.38 (95% CI: 12.27– 205 16.22) years old, respectively ( Fig. 3c ). In the sporadic case (SP139, 72 years old), the 206 acquisition of APC T1274Qfs*9 occurred at about 30.59 (95% CI: 23.28–38.38) years old ( Fig. 3d ), 207 over 40 years prior to diagnosis. Somatic APC was indeed associated with clonal expansions 208 according to the single-crypt phylogenetic trees. However, we also observed non-AKP driver-209 associated clonal expansions, including CACNA1A and FBLN2 in SP139, and FAT1 and 210 

TCF7L2 in FAP04. 211 

Non-AKP clones contribute to polyclonal origins in sporadic polyps 212 

The polyclonal origin of human colorectal premalignant tissues has recently been demonstrated 213 as a new paradigm 18,19 . We therefore asked how AKP and non-AKP clones contribute to 214 polyclonality in sporadic and FAP polyps. To this end, we reconstructed single-crypt phylogenies 215 for each tissue, along with matched normal crypts from the same patient (Fig. 4a-b, 216 

Supplementary Fig. 6 ). A clonality score was defined based on polygenetic topology to quantify 217 the clonality and distinguish polyclonal (score <0.05) from monoclonal (score >=0.05) polyps 218 (Methods ). 219 Indeed, most premalignant polyps (85% or 22 out of 26) were composed of multiple clonal 220 lineages and had a low clonality score ( Fig. 4a-b, Supplementary Figs. 6-7 ), supporting their 221 polyclonal origins. Interestingly, polyclonal origin was more common in FAP than in sporadic 222 polyps ( Fig. 4c ). This is likely due to sporadic polyps being diagnosed later than FAP, as 223 reflected by the more irregular morphology of single crypts in sporadic polyps compared to FAP 224 polyps ( Fig. 2d-e ). Polyclonal tissues harbored fewer somatic mutations than monoclonal ones, 225 consistent with progressive polyclonal-to-monoclonal evolution 19 (Fig. 4d ). The fraction of non-226 AKP crypts within a tissue negatively correlated with the clonality score ( Fig. 4e ), indicating that 227 non-AKP clones were strongly enriched in polyclonal tissues. This pattern was more prominent 228 in sporadic polyclonal tissues, with a substantial fraction of non-AKP crypts (10–100%; Fig. 4f ). 229 In contrast, FAP polyclonal tissues were often characterized by parallel evolution of distinct 230

Monoclonal transition recapitulates Big-Bang-like growth in colorectal precancer 243 

Since the spatial information where each crypt was dissected from the tissue was recorded, we 244 next performed a phylogeographic analysis to distinguish spatial lineage segregation from 245 lineage intermixing. Spatial segregation refers to the scenario where phylogenetically close 246 crypts are located in neighboring regions, as exemplified by sporadic (SP130P) and FAP 247 (FAP03P4) cases ( Fig. 5a , Supplementary Figs. 8-9 ). In contrast, spatial lineage intermixing 248 refers to the scenario where phylogenetically close crypts are found in spatially distant regions, 249 as exemplified by a sporadic case (SP131P) and an FAP (FAP01P6) case (Fig. 5b ,250 

Supplementary Figs. 8-9 ). In these tissues, the presence of the same low-VAF somatic 251 mutations across distant regions further supported spatial lineage intermixing (Fig. 5c, 252 

Supplementary Fig. 9c-d ).253 Interestingly, we found that the pattern of spatial lineage segregation versus intermixing was 254 strongly associated with the tissue clonality (polyclonal versus monoclonal) (Fig. 5d ). 255 Specifically, spatial lineage intermixing was more prevalent in monoclonal polyps (75% or 3 out 256 of 4) than in polyclonal polyps (27.3% or 3 out of 11) ( Fig. 5d ). Polyps with spatial lineage 257 intermixing carried significantly more somatic mutations at the single-crypt level than polyps with 258 spatial lineage segregation ( Fig. 5e ), indicating that intermixing marks a more advanced 259 evolutionary stage. Notably, most malignant CRCs (75%, 3 of 4) in our cohort also exhibited 260 spatial lineage intermixing ( Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 10 ). Since spatial lineage 261 intermixing is a defining feature of Big-Bang model of tumor growth 31 , our data indicate that Big-262 Bang-like growth can begin as early as the premalignant stage. 263 To further verify spatial lineage intermixing as a key feature of Big-Bang tumor growth, we 264 simulated the two-dimensional spatial growth of virtual tumors. Then, we sampled individual 265 crypts from multiple regions to reconstruct single-crypt phylogenetic trees, mimicking our multi-266 regional single-crypt sampling strategy ( Methods ). Our simulations revealed strong spatial 267 segregation for the early initiated clones through polyclonal origins, followed by a selective 268 sweep of a dominant clone ( Fig. 5f ). Importantly, this clonal sweep generates the characteristic 269 spatial intermixing pattern as observed in the monoclonal premalignant tissues and CRCs ( Fig. 270 

5f-h and Supplementary Fig. 11 ). Together, our phylogeographic analysis and simulations 271 revealed that spatial lineage architecture is mechanistically linked to the disease stage, and that 272 the polyclonal-to-monoclonal transition in precancerous tissues aligns with Big-Bang-like tumor 273 growth. 274

Discussion 275 

Although both sporadic and hereditary colorectal precancers often arise through polyclonal 276 origins, our single-crypt WGS analysis shows that they initiate and evolve through distinct 277 genomic routes. Sporadic polyps frequently develop not by the canonical driver genes (e.g. APC ,278 

KRAS and TP53 ), but instead through non-AKP driver mutations including KMT2C, CACNA1A 279 

and FBLN2 . In contrast, the second hit on the remaining allele of APC in FAP lesions contributes 280 to polyclonal initiation, where most premalignant crypts hit by a second APC mutation or 5q LOH 281 at a strikingly early in life (often prior to birth). These support the two distinct molecular 282 trajectories underlying the same mode of precancer polyclonality observed in both sporadic and 283 hereditary conditions. 284 Canonical CRC drivers, including APC , KRAS , and TP53 , are thought to be essential for 285 tumorigenesis; however, one important implication of our findings is that early polyclonal 286 expansion in polyps not necessarily require those CRC drivers. In sporadic lesions, non-AKP 287 crypts also showed dysplastic morphology and high mutation burdens comparable to those of 288 

APC -mutant crypts. This suggests that such clones are not passive bystanders, but active 289 participant in lesion clonal expansion. One plausible model is that these mutations act as “mini-290 drivers”: individually modest, but sufficient to disrupt epithelial homeostasis, alter cell 291 competition, or reshape local microenvironmental interactions 6,19,50,51 . Their functions might 292 require specific timing, such as the short time window before monoclonal sweep. Our data 293 therefore expand the current model of colorectal tumor initiation from a strictly gene-centric 294 sequence to a broader ecological and evolutionary process. 295 Tumor growth and progression involve the breakdown of normal tissue organization 52 . Previous 296 studies showed the Big Bang model and exposes variegation in carcinomas but not adenomas 31 .297 However, we found Big-Bang growth occurs early at monoclonal transition of polyps, exhibiting 298 a spatially mixed architecture and substantial variegated branch mutations. Notably, early 299 polyclonal polyps display a spatially segregated pattern, occasionally accompanied by local 300 monophyletic clusters, suggesting selective advantages in clonal competition may be the basis 301 under field cancerization. These observations recapitulate predictions of a Big-Bang growth and 302 directly support the concept that tumors are “born-to-be-bad”. This underscore that tumor-303 initiating events in colorectal cancer may occur much earlier than previously recognized, which 304 may help to better inform strategies for early detection, risk stratification, and prevention before 305 these morphologically normal crypts transform into neoplasia. Collectively, our study defines 306 distinct genomic trajectories of early colorectal tumorigenesis in sporadic and hereditary 307 contexts, providing a conceptual framework for understanding how these lesions diverge during 308 initiation and converge when out. Our findings support a view in which precancer initiation is 309 shaped by both somatic mutation and tissue organization. The former is reflected by the distinct 310 driver landscapes of sporadic and FAP lesions, whereas the latter is captured by the spatial 311 restructuring that accompanies the transition from polyclonal to monoclonal stage. 312 These new insights may also inform clinical practice in early cancer interception. As monoclonal 313 polyps with Big-Bang-like lineage mixing may mark a more advanced and potentially higher-risk 314 stage of disease, defining the biomarkers of this stage would greatly help guide early 315 intervention. Colorectal cancer also develops through other routes, including Lynch syndrome 316 and colitis-associated neoplasia in inflammatory bowel disease 11 . Defining the evolutionary 317 trajectories of these additional pathways will complete our understanding of CRC initiation 318 across multiple disease subtypes and guide earlier detection and prevention. 319

Methods 320 

Sample collection 321 

Our human colorectal precancer cohort consisted of nine patients (six sporadic and three with 322 FAP) ( Supplementary Table 1 ), recruited from the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen 323 University. All of the sporadic patients had synchronous polyps and CRC located at distinct sites 324 of the colon. Of the three patients with FAP, two had developed CRC by the time tissue samples 325 were collected. All biospecimen collection protocols were conducted in accordance with the 326 principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Institutional Review Board 327 (IRB) of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (2019ZSLYEC-06). All enrolled 328 patients had signed informed consent forms, and all samples were anonymously coded in 329 accordance with local ethical guidelines. In total, we have obtained 28 polyps (P), 9 synchronous 330 colorectal tumors (T), and 17 adjacent normal tissues (N) from these 9 patients ( Supplementary 331 

Table 1 ). Of these 54 samples, 46 samples, including normal mucosa (n = 16), premalignant 332 polyps (n = 26), and synchronous malignant adenocarcinomas (n = 4), were subject to single-333 crypt isolation and WGS, while the remaining 8 samples were subject to tissue-level bulk WES 334 (Supplementary Table 1 ). 335 The bulk tissue samples were immediately frozen and then subjected to histopathological 336 assessment, single-crypt dissection, WGS, and/or tissue-level bulk WES. For the 337 histopathological assessment, the sample tissues were embedded in an Optimal Cutting 338 Temperature (OCT) compound (Sakura Finetek, USA) using pre-cooled molds. Then, 339 cryosectioning, fixation, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining were conducted. 340 

Single-crypt isolation and morphology assessment 341 

We adapted the single-crypt isolation procedure from the published protocols 19,30 (Fig. 1b ). First, 342 four to eight spatially distinct regions were dissected from each tissue ( Supplementary Fig. 1 ). 343 Then, the dissected tissues were placed on a clean glass slide under the microscope and 344 carefully added 20 μL pre-cold PBS. Next, individual crypts (or glands) were manually 345 dissociated using a 23G (0.6 x 25 mm) injection needle and aspirated using a pipette. Each 346 crypt was then transferred to a PCR tube containing 20 μL of proteinase K buffer. 347 Based on microscopic morphology, crypts obtained through microdissection can be classified 348 as irregular crypts 53 if they meet at least one of the following criteria: 1) crypts exhibiting 349 significant enlargement, including elongation and increased diameter. Specifically, the length of 350 an irregular crypt is approximately twice that of adjacent normal crypts 54 , and the diameter of 351 irregular crypts in aberrant crypt foci (ACF) measure is up to 1.5 times greater than that of a 352 normal crypt 55 ; 2) Crypt basal dilatation ≥ 10% of crypts 56 ; 3) Irregular crypts displaying double 353 or triple branching crypts, or other pronounced forms of budding and branching 54-56 ; 4) A peculiar 354 growth pattern involving the horizontal orientation of deep crypts that seem to grow parallel to 355 the muscularis mucosae, often creating an inverted T- or L-shaped crypt 56 .356 

Single-crypt whole genome sequencing 357 

Genomic DNA was extracted from isolated crypts by following a published protocol 33 . First, each 358 crypt was transferred to a pre-loaded 200 μL PCR tube containing 10 μL of lysis buffer (30 μL 359 Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 5 μL Tween-20, 5 μL IGEPAL CA-630, 50 μL 25 ug/mL proteinase K, and 360 910 μL ddH 2O). The tubes were then incubated at 50 °C for 12 minutes. Somatic copy number alterations and LOH analysis 430 

SCNA, ploidy, and tumor purity for single-crypt or tissue-level bulk lesions were estimated using 431 TitanCNA 60 , a hidden Markov model-based method. For each patient, we identified germline 432 heterozygous SNP at dbSNP 146 loci in the normal biopsy using SAMtools and SnpEff. Read 433 counts for 10,000 base pair (bp) bins across the genome were generated for all samples using 434 HMMcopy. Then, TitanCNA was used to determine the allelic ratios at germline heterozygous 435 SNP loci in normal, precancerous and cancerous crypts and the depth ratios between crypts 436 and control normal samples in the bins containing those SNP loci. Nine TitanCNA runs with nine 437 combinations of subclone number, purity, and ploidy were performed. Different numbers of 438 subclones (n = 1, 2 or 3), purities (n = 0.2, 0.4, or 0.6), and ploidy (2) were set. One run was 439 selected for each tumor biopsy based on manual inspection of the fitted results. Results with a 440 single subclone were prioritized unless results with multiple subclones better fit the data visually. 441 Notably, assuming the crypt as a single clone, we also re-adjusted the output using an in-house 442 script. SCNAs were identified in single-crypts relative to paired normal tissue-level bulk tissues. 443 For amplification, the absolute copy number was greater than 2.8, and the copy number relative 444 to median ploidy was greater than 0.8. For deletions, the absolute copy number was less than 445 1.2 and copy number relative to median ploidy was less than -0.8. The neutral LOH regions 446 were called using TitanCNA. Mutational signature extraction and assignment 458 

Mutational signatures were identified using previously reported piplines 61 . First, a hierarchical 459 Dirichlet process (HDP v0.1.5) 37 was used without priors to extract the mutational signatures. 460 We set the alpha and beta to one for the alpha clustering parameter. Gibbs sampler was ran 461 with 20,000 burn-in iterations (parameter ‘burnin’). With a spacing of 1000 iterations (parameter 462 ‘space’), 100 iterations were collected (parameter ‘n’). After each Gibbs sampling iteration, three 463 iterations of concentration parameter sampling (parameter ‘cpiter’) were performed. 464 For identified mutational signatures, only the signatures with ≥0.9 cosine similarity with the 465 reference as the same signatures were considered. For the remaining signatures, HDP was ran 466 with all known PCAWG reference signatures V3.1 62 as priors and kept the extracted signatures 467 as a shortlist of candidate reference signatures. To deconvolute composite signatures and to 468 equate obtained HDP signatures to reference signatures, we used an expectation-maximization 469 algorithm to deconstruct these signatures into reference constituents. We ran a second round 470 of expectation maximization that only kept reference signatures with >10% contributions for 471 each HDP signature to reduce overfitting. The final SBS mutational signatures permitted in each 472 patient were the corresponding deconvoluted reference signatures for HDP components 473 contributing to at least 5% of mutations in at least one sample. This amounted to reference 474 signatures SBS1, SBS2, SBS5, SBS13, SBS18, SBS88 for normal crypt and SBS1, SBS2, 475 SBS3, SBS5, SBS13, SBS18, SBS88, SBS3, SBS17a, SBS17b for polyp and tumor crypts. The 476 final proportion of reference signatures was assigned to each crypt using sigfit (v2.2) 38 .477 

Somatic LINE-1 retrotransposition analysis 478 

The LINE1 (L1) retrotranspositions was called using DELLY 46 and xTea 45 with matched tissue-479 level bulk normal samples and single crypts to detect somatic insertions. Potential germline calls, 480 overlapping with events found in matched tissue-level bulk samples, were removed. 481 For DELLY, we excluded rearrangements with poor mapping quality (median MAPQ < 40), 482 insufficient supporting reads, or excess discordant reads in matched normal BAM files. We also 483 removed short deletions and duplications (<1 kb) lacking soft-clipped reads and unbalanced 484 inversions <5 kb with fewer than five supporting reads, as these are typically library artifacts. 485 Breakpoint positions and microhomology sequences were refined using SA tags from soft-486 clipped reads. Breakpoint variant allele fraction was estimated from discordant read pairs 487 supporting the rearrangement relative to concordant read pairs supporting the wild-type 488 configuration. 489 For xTea, insertion-calling thresholds were adjusted according to insertion clonality and 490 sequencing depth. Candidate insertions with few or no support in the matched normal sample 491 were classified as somatic. Insertions overlapping repetitive regions from the same TE family 492 were removed when the reference repeat showed low divergence from the RepeatMasker 493 consensus. xTea then required consistent clustering of 5 ′ and 3 ′ clipped reads and their 494 corresponding discordant mates within the expected insert-size range. Candidates supported 495 on only one side of the breakpoint were further resolved using discordant reads or local read-496 depth patterns to identify transductions or target-site deletions. 497

Spatial tumor growth simulation and region-based lineage sampling. a, Rep-resentative snapshots of the two-dimensional lattice-based tumor growth simulation at 12 selected time points, illustrating the spatial expansion and clonal diversification of tumor cells over time. Each panel shows the full 100×100 lattice, with colors indicating distinct clones. b, Schematic of the spatial sampling strategy used to construct lineage trees from the simulated tumor.
