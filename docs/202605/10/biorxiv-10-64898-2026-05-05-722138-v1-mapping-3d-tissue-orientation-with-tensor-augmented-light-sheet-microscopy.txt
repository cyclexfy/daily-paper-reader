Abstract 23 

Mapping the three -dimensional directional organization of biological tissues is 24 

essential for understanding their structure and function. However, existing 25 

methods cannot resolve micrometer -scale orientation in volumetric samples. 26 

We introduce tensor light -sheet scattering microscopy (tLSSM), a label -free 27 

method that reconstructs 3D fiber orientations at micrometer resolution in 28 

optically cleared tissues, enabling whole -organ imaging. We discovered that 29 

even in transparent samples, organized structures such as neural fibers scatter 30 

light in directional patterns, consistent with models of light scattering by 31 

cylinders. We compared tLSSM against diffusion MRI in the mouse brain, 32 

demonstrating strong agreement and orders of magnitude superior spatial 33 

resolution. Furthermore, we showcase tLSSM’s versatility across diverse 34 

contexts, including whole -brain label -free tracing, pathological demyelination 35 

lesions, heart tissue, peripheral nerves, and human white matter. By enabling 36 

whole -organ fiber orientation mapping compatible with standard light -sheet 37 

microscopes, tLSSM establishes a new standard for mesoscopic connectivity 38 

studies by mapping tissue architecture beyond the limits of traditional 39 

sectioning. 40

1.  Introduction 41 

Biological tissues are characterized by anisotropic microarchitectures, where the spatial 42 

orientation of cells and extracellular matrix components underpins physiological function. In 43 

the brain, neural circuits form a complex network with directional organization spanning scales 44 

from macroscopic inter -regional connectivity to microscopic axonal pathways 1. This large -45 

scale organization, termed macroconnectomics or projectomics, provides insights into how 46 

brain areas communicate and coordinate to support cognitive, behavioral and other functional 47 

processes 2.48 

Mapping neural circuits remains a fundamental challenge. Early approaches, such as 49 

invasive tract -tracing, have enabled the construction of mesoscopic connectivity atlases from 50 

thousands of brains, such as the Allen Mouse Brain Connectivity Atlas 3. However, these 51 

approaches based on one tracer injection per characterized tract are limited in scalability. 52 

Conversely, methods requiring no staining of tracers (i.e., label -free methods), like diffusion -53 

weighted magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI), offer non -invasive, non -destructive 54 

tractography but suffer from a structural resolution limit 4. Clinical dMRI voxels are typically 55 

millimeter -scale 5, and even high -resolution ex -vivo setups remain orders of magnitude larger 56 

than individual axons 6–8. This discrepancy leads to ill -posed orientation estimates and false -57 

positive tract  delineations 9,10 .58 

Efforts to bridge this gap have largely relied on tissue sectioning. Microscopic techniques 59 

such as polarized light imaging (PLI) 11,12 , serial optical coherence tomography (SOCT) 13,14 ,60 

and computational scattered light imaging (ComSLI) 15,16  provide in -plane 2D orientation. 61 

Others, like polarization -sensitive OCT (PS -OCT) 17 , advancements on  PLI 18,19 , or permittivity 62 

tensor imaging ( PTI) 20 , offer 3D orientation information but remain limited to histological 63 

slices. Small -angle X -ray scattering tensor tomography (SAXS -TT) 21  reveals 3D  orientation of 64 

myelinated fibers in whole -mount (intact) specimens 22 , but high -resolution imaging is 65

constrained by long acquisition times. Moreover, SAXS -TT and many of these techniques rely 66 

on myelin being intact, which may be compromised in pathological conditions. 67 

Light -sheet fluorescence microscopy (LSFM) enables 3D volumetric imaging at micrometer 68 

resolution in intact optically cleared tissues. Clearing techniques homogenize refractive indices 69 

across tissue components (e.g., proteins, lipids, water), reducing light scattering and enabling 70 

deep imaging. LSFM has predominantly relied on fluorescent stains to visualize specific 71 

structures 23 –25 . Recently, alternative light -sheet based studies have reported elastic scattering 72 

as a candidate for label -free imaging of small samples 26 , referring to the method as light -sheet 73 

tomography (LST), light -sheet scattering microscopy (LSSM), or scattered light -sheet 74 

microscopy (sLS). Elastic scattering originates from local refractive index variations, and it 75 

involves photons that are deflected without loss of energy. Elastic scattering provides 76 

information about the sample architecture across different scales: below the wavelength of 77 

incident light (Rayleigh scattering) and scales comparable to and larger than the wavelength 78 

(referred to as Mie scattering in biomedical  optics) 27,28 .79 

Here, we present a 3D light -sheet scattering -based method for multiview mesoscopic 80 

imaging, enabling label -free, micrometer -resolution mapping of 3D fiber orientation in intact, 81 

optically cleared tissues. In this work, we refer to the acquisition of scattering data from 82 

multiple sample orientations as multiview light -sheet scattering microscopy (mvLSSM), and 83 

the subsequent directional reconstruction as tensor light -sheet scattering microscopy (tLSSM). 84 

We demonstrate the application of tLSSM in the whole mouse brain, where we report that 85 

scattered light from neural fibers reveals an orientation -dependent signal, consistent with prior 86 

observations on collagen fibers 29 . To capture directional scattering, we developed an imaging 87 

workflow combining sample rotation, multiview acquisition, and 3D registratio n, allowing 88 

reconstruction of voxel -wise tissue orientation from multiple illumination angles. We 89

2.  Results 98 

2.1 . Scattered light -sheet microscopy reveals anisotropic signal in the brain 99 

To characterize how the LSSM signal relates to the orientation of anisotropic tissue, we 100 

imaged iDISCO+ -cleared mouse whole -brains at 4.5 μm isotropic voxel size using a dual -view, 101 

double -sided macroSPIM system (see Methods, Supplementary Methods 1 and Supplementary 102 

Figure 1 for instrument description). 103 

Each imaging plane was acquired from four perspectives —LF, RF, LB, and RB —identified 104 

by light -sheet origin (left, L or right, R) and camera position (front, F or back, B). Unlike 105 

conventional LSFM, which assumes fluorescence is emitted isotropically 30 , LSSM reveals a 106 

structured, orientation -dependent signal absent in autofluorescence. This signal intensity is 107 

modulated by the light -sheet's angle of origin, resembling neural pathways (Fig. 1b -e). 108 

To assess the anatomical relevance of the LSSM signal, we performed whole -mount 109 

immunofluorescence for myelin basic protein (MBP), a marker of myelinated axons forming 110 

the pathways. We observed that a single light -sheet perspective could not capture the full 111 

trajectory of the pathways (Fig. 1h -m and Extended Data Fig. 1). Instead, complementary 112 

patterns emerged across opposing perspectives (Fig. 1c -e), and similarities appeared between 113

2.2  Characterization of the  directional scattering signatures of  the 3D neural 154 

pathways 155 

To characterize scattering signals across  different positions in a cleared mouse brain, we 156 

acquired an image series of volumetric  data across a full 360° in -plane  rotation range in 157 

nominal 20° steps (i.e., a mvLSSM dataset). The  resulting 3D volumes were registered to a 158 

common space using an in -house algorithm (Supplementary Methods 4). The intensity changes 159 

with rotation steps were particularly evident in white matter structures, such as the corpus 160 

callosum and internal capsule (Fig. 2 a,b , see also Supplementary Videos 1,2,3 ). 161 

Voxel -wise scattering signal profiles were extracted from the image series (Fig. 2c -g). In 162 

white matter regions containing densely packed axons, such as the corpus callosum and 163 

fimbria, the profiles showed two peaks approximately 180° apart  (179.98 ± 1.57°), reflecting 164 

the symmetry of fiber scattering (Fig. 2c -e). In contrast, gray matter regions such as the cortex 165 

and thalamus displayed broader, less distinct peaks, likely reflecting the absence of a 166 

predominant direction, yielding a more isotropic signal. Still, the 180° periodicity could be 167 

appreciated (Fig. 2f,g). 168 

To quantify peak position and its prominence, we used the principal component analysis 169 

(PCA) algorithm. PCA was applied voxel -wise on a  3D space (Fig. 2c -e, bottom row), where 170 

each data point represents the applied rotation to the sample and the measured intensity in each 171 

voxel. The first eigenvector represents the applied rotation to measure a peak (Fig. 2h). 172 

Notably, scattering peak positions acquired from opposing  light -sheet -camera pairs (LF/RB vs. 173 

RF/LB) were consistently offset by 90° (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). The PCA first eigenvalue 174 

(Fig. 2i), representing peak amplitude, was highest in white matter regions (cyan arrows) 175 

independently of their orientation, highlighting densely packed axon bundles such as the corpus 176 

callosum and caudoputamen striale fibers, respectively. The second eigenvalue (Fig. 2j), 177 

sensitive to baseline intensity and peak width, showed patterns similar to those of the first 178

2.3 Modeling the 3D fiber orientation using infinite -cylinder scattering theory 217 

To reconstruct the 3D fiber orientations from a mvLSSM dataset, we modeled light 218 

scattering from fibers as infinite cylinders, parameterized by two angles (Fig. 3a): the in -plane 219 

azimuthal orientation ( βf) and the out -of -plane tilt ( αf). Theoretical predictions based on 220 

Maxwell’s equations indicate that light is scattered along a cone centered on the fiber axis, with 221 

the cone’s aperture determined by the angle between the incident light propagation direction 222 

and the fiber orientation (Extended Data Fig. 3a -f, Supplementary Methods 3, Supplementary 223 

Video  4)32 .224 

We first simulated scattering profiles for the macroSPIM configuration, where the rotation 225 

axis (Y -axis) is perpendicular to both the illumination (X -axis) and detection axes (Z -axis). 226 

When simulating a sample rotation, we are effectively changing the in -plane,  β, orientation of 227 

the fiber. The first insight is that  a peak only appears when the in -plane orientation component 228 

is  β=45° (Fig. 3b). On the other hand, the peak amplitude is expected to vary with tilting  (αf >229 

0°), such that out -of -plane fibers show lower peak amplitudes than in -plane fibers (Fig. 3c). 230 

When the fiber is aligned with the rotation axis ( αf=0 °), the scattering intensity profile is 231 

constant. All these insights are aligned with the experimental observations in the sciatic horse 232 

nerve study (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2). 233 

While our study is focused on the macroSPIM system, we also assess other light -sheet 234 

hardware configurations. We simulated the scattering profiles for the ultramicroscope 235 

configuration, where the detection is placed on the top of the sample. The effective difference 236

Nonetheless, we still observed some changes in the peak prominence (reflected in the 2D -FA 245

the rotation axis ( αf=0) in one of the two mountings. For example, in the posterior cingulum 250 

(Fig. 3d, region of interest 1), the 2D -FA was noticeably larger in one of the mountings than 251 

the other. 252 

To better understand the limitations of the amplitude modulation, we simulated potential 253 

confounding factors, such as variations in fiber diameter or orientation distributions (Extended 254 

Data Fig.  3g-j). The simulations confirmed that such features could influence the amplitude 255 

modulation, complicating its use as a reliable proxy for fiber orientation. Other factors, such as 256 

fiber density 33 , or practical differences with the theoretical model, such as light attenuation, the 257 

angular dispersion on the incident illumination or the numerical aperture in detection, may 258 

further spread the scattering peaks and reduce orientation specificity. 259

2.4 Two -planes acquisition resolves the complete 3D fiber orientation 261 

To robustly recover full 3D fiber orientation, we combined two mvLSSM  datasets, with the 262 

second obtained after manual sample rotation (~75°) about an axis in the XZ plane (Fig. 3d). 263 

This yields two independent in -plane orientation measurements,  β and  β′, from which the out -264 

of -plane tilt ( αf) can be estimated via triangulation avoiding the use of the amplitude 265 

modulation in a single rotation -plane dataset. 266 

Simulations confirmed that any non -zero manual rotation between mvLSSM datasets 267 

enables the estimation of  αf (Fig. 3f). However, a small subset of fiber tilting orientations 268 

remains ambiguous depending on the second mounting orientation (Methods; Supplementary 269 

Video  5). For example, for Z -axis rotation between mountings, fibers near the XY plane ( βf ≈270 

0°/180° ) are affected by tilting uncertainty. The subset of affected fibers decreased the closer 271 

the manual rotation was to 90°. Still, we observed that <5% of orientations would be affected 272 

for inter -acquisition rotation angles >40°, well below the >75° used here (Fig. 3f). 273 

We also evaluated the acquisition rotation step ( Δω ). Simulations using observed white -274 

matter peak widths (~40°) showed that  Δω  = 20° balances accuracy and acquisition time, 275 

yielding mean  βf errors <5% (Fig. 3g). Higher uncertainty occurs for fibers aligned with the 276 

rotation axis (flat profiles) or with large tilt ( αf ≈ 90°) due to narrower peaks, respectively. 277 

Experiments with  Δω  = 10° and 20° produced consistent results across white -matter ROIs 278 

(mean difference 2.00 ± 2.06°; Extended Data Fig. 5). 279

mvLSSM datasets (i.e., tLSSM) and well -established diffusion tensor MRI on the same brain. 303

3.  Discussion 395 

We demonstrated that the intrinsic scattering properties of optically cleared tissues 396 

can be decoded to map 3D fiber orientation at micrometer scale in whole, intact organs. 397 

By developing tensor light -sheet scattering microscopy (tLSSM), we addressed the 398 

resolution limits inherent to dMRI 4 while avoiding the scalability constraints of tracer -399 

based or sectioning methods 2. We showed the versatility of tLSSM across diverse 400 

biological architectures, including the intact mouse brain and heart, horse sciatic nerve, 401 

and human white matter. As a result, we have established a scalable and non‑destructive 402 

framework for mapping anisotropic tissue architecture, enabling the virtual tracing of 403 

whole‑brain projections at the micron axon -fascicle level and offering sensitive markers 404 

for microstructural pathology. 405 

To assess the robustness of the method, we compared tLSSM with dMRI fiber 406 

orientation maps on the same mouse brain (~0.5 cm³). We found that overall orientation 407 

patterns are stable across modalities and robust to averaging artifacts when a voxel 408 

contains multiple tissue types (i.e., partial‑volume effects) 1. While we observed strong 409 

hemispheric symmetry and closely matched diffusion tensor MRI –derived and tLSSM 410 

orientation maps (Fig. 4a -d), we achieved an approximately 2 1,400 -fold ( ~28³) increase 411 

in volumetric resolution, enabling us to reveal complex architectural details that are 412 

blurred at dMRI scales 4. For example, we clearly resolved striatal axon fascicles in the 413 

caudate and putamen and detected fascicular projections into brainstem pathways that 414 

are not visible with MRI (Fig. 4a -l). Similarly, we captured anatomically relevant 415 

variations in gray matter anisotropy, such as the mouse barrel cortex (Fig. 4m). This 416 

fidelity enabled us to perform label -free, streamline -based tractography in individual 417 

brains that closely resembled the tracer -based maps of the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas 3.418 

The achievable image resolution of tLSSM is primarily limited by the optical field of 419

Beyond orientation mapping, we leveraged the sensitivity of subcellular structures to 445 

detect microstructural alterations in focal demyelinating lesions. Voxel -level anisotropy 446 

reflects an ensemble average of the scattering profiles from a mixture of microstructural 447 

compartments such as cells and axons. Consequently, cellular -dominated regions like the 448 

cerebral cortex exhibit a more isotropic scattering profile. This aligns with observations 449 

in diffusion MRI, despite the fundamental difference in probing mechanisms (light 450 

scattering vs. water diffusion). This averaging effect also elucidates signal changes in 451 

pathology; in focal demyelinating lesions of the mouse brain, we observed reduced 452 

anisotropic scattering (Fig. 5a -d). This is possibly driven not only by myelin loss but also 453 

by an inflammatory response characterized by increased density of infiltrating glial 454 

cells 35 , which introduces isotropic scattering signal —a mechanism supported by recent 455 

synchrotron and MRI imaging studies at 0.5 and 200 μm resolution, respectively 1,47 .456 

We identified the sample preparation protocol as a critical factor for preservation of 457 

the scattering signal. We observed that while the scattering signal is robust across 458 

solvent - (iDISCO+, BABB) and water -based (CUBIC) clearing protocols, and 459 

compatible with immunofluorescence labeling (MBP staining), it is sensitive to fixation 460 

chemistry. The scattering signal was preserved in PFA -based perfusion fixed tissues but 461 

reduced in glyoxal -fixed samples. While the underlying mechanism requires further 462 

study, we hypothesize that the distinct protein cross -linking network formed by PFA may 463 

be essential for preserving the refractive index fluctuations that generate the scattering 464 

contrast 48 .465 

Furthermore, we showed the generalizability of the theoretical framework of tLSSM 466 

to other light -sheet microscopy configurations. However, the reconstruction requires 467 

accounting for the specific geometry of the system; for example, the use of different 468 

orthogonal systems (e.g., macroSPIM vs. Ultramicroscope) is conceptually equivalent to 469

a non -destructive, label -free modality compatible with fluorescence, tLSSM could be 481

We anticipate that tLSSM will enable the development of multimodal atlases across 486 

diverse organ systems, providing a versatile tool to unravel the complex relationship 487 

between microscopic tissue anisotropy and molecular function in health and disease. 488

4.7 Light scattering by infinite cylinders modeling 598

a plane electromagnetic wave with wavelength shorter than the cylinder length is well 601 

described 32,60  (see Supplementary Methods 3 for a  detailed  description). When light  is 602 

incident  on an infinite -long cylinder, the scattered light propagates along the surface of a 603 

cone, with its apex at the point of incidence. The cone’s half -angle is given by π/2  - φ, 604 

which matches the angle between the incident light direction and the fiber orientation. 605 

The solution  to  the scattering problem requires identifying the scattered field in any 606

total intensity in each point of the scattered cone depends on the elevation angle, φ; and 608 

the scattering angle, θ. The angle φ is related to the aperture of the cone of scattered light, 609 

and θ identifies an angle around the cylinder’s cone, i.e., it identifies a particular 610 

propagation direction within the cone. 611
