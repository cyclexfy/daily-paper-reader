# Abstract 20 

Identifying cancer driver genes and their therapeutic impact remains a core challenge in 21 

computational cancer biology. We introduce xNNDriver and xAEDriver, two interpretable neural 22 

network frameworks that connect cancer mutations with genome-wide DepMap gene 23 

dependencies, pathway activity, and drug-response patterns. xNNDriver is a supervised pathway-24 

guided model that evaluates whether a gene’s mutation status is encoded in the genome-wide 25 

dependency landscape; we interpret model fitness as a driver potential score, which quantifies the 26 

strength of this mutation-dependency signal and prioritizes genes with broad functional footprints. 27 

Across 3,008 candidate genes, xNNDriver recovers major established drivers and highlights 28 

literature-supported candidates, while pathway analyses reveal biologically coherent programs 29 

related to metabolism, growth factor signaling, and immune regulation. To capture combinatorial 30 

functional states, xAEDriver uses an unsupervised autoencoder to learn Driver Variant 31 

Representations (DVRs), latent binary features guided by the frequency distribution of known 32 

driver mutations. DVRs capture cell-line-specific dependency patterns and expression patterns and 33 

are associated with drug sensitivity and pathway activity. Together, these interpretable deep 34 

learning models demonstrate that gene dependency landscapes encode rich, interpretable signals 35 

of oncogenic function and provide a hypothesis-generating framework for prioritizing drivers, 36 

pathways, and therapeutic vulnerabilities for further experimental validation. 37

# Introduction 52 

One of the most critical and challenging areas in cancer biology is the identification of driver genes 53 

and mutations. Cancer driver mutations fuel tumor progression, unlike neutral passenger mutations 54 

(1). In recent years, machine learning and deep learning have shown great potential in 55 

bioinformatics, including applications for detecting cancer driver genes and mutations (2-6). 56 

However, current deep learning models predominantly utilize sequence data and three-dimensional 57 

protein structures to infer driver mutations (1), while functional genomics, especially gene editing, 58 

remains less explored. Additionally, the interpretability of these black-box models is often limited. 59 

Conventional gene perturbation strategies, such as knockout and RNAi-mediated knockdown, 60 

enable researchers to study a gene’s loss-of-function effects on tumor progression, aiding in 61 

identifying cancer-driver genes and mutations (7). CRISPR-Cas9 has advanced gene perturbation 62 

in terms of precision and scale (8). The Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) at the Broad Institute 63

(9, 10), providing gene dependency scores that reflect each gene’s importance for cell proliferation 65 

and survival in a cell-line-specific manner. While DepMap dependency scores provide valuable 66 

insights into the essentiality of genes in cancer cells, they are not sufficient on their own to 67 

conclusively determine whether a gene is a driver of cancer. Accurate identification of cancer 68 

driver genes requires an integrated analysis of dependency scores with other data types, such as 69 

DNA mutations, pathway involvement, and gene expression. 70 

We hypothesize that the mutations with driver activity can leave detectable signatures in the 71 

genome-wide dependency landscape, whereas passenger mutations are less likely to produce 72 

coherent dependency shifts. To test the hypothesis, we built a supervised learning model to predict 73 

the mutation status (mutated or not) of each candidate gene, using the dependency scores of all 74 

screened genes in a cell line. The model’s fitness, expressed as the driver potential score of this 75 

candidate gene, prioritizes genes whose mutation status has a strong functional footprint in 76 

dependency data. We name this model xNNDriver (e xplainable Neural Network for Driver gene 77 

identification). Because the labels are derived from existing mutation annotations, xNNDriver is 78 

best viewed as a functional prioritization and interpretation framework rather than an independent 79 

de novo mutation-discovery tool. Our approach successfully identified both well-known driver 80 

genes and novel candidates. To discern the pathways through which these drivers contribute to 81 

tumorigenesis, we incorporated the Reactome pathway hierarchy (11) into the network 82 

architecture, designing each neuron to represent a distinct biological pathway. 83

autoencoder model that integrates both dependency scores and gene expression data. This 85

## The fitness of the supervised xNNDriver quantifies cancer driver 96 

## potential from dependency landscapes 97 

Our supervised xNNDriver model links genome-wide gene dependency scores to the mutation 98 

status of a candidate driver gene in each cancer cell line. The mutation status of a true driver gene, 99 

but not passenger mutations, influences the impact of other genes’ knockout on tumor survival 100 

(dependency). In other words, the distinct genome-wide dependency scores can predict the 101 

mutation status of driver genes (high model fitness), but not those of passenger genes (low model 102 

fitness). We define the resulting model fitness as the driver potential score, a measure of mutation-103 

dependency association and functional prioritization rather than direct proof of causality. A 104 

schematic overview of the workflow is shown in Fig 1A. 105

To construct the training data, we determined each candidate gene’s mutation status based on the 120 

presence of functional SNP somatic mutations, as annotated by both the COSMIC (12) and 121 

ClinVar (13) databases. The model was trained using a biologically informed feedforward neural 122 

network whose architecture mirrors the pathway hierarchy acquired from the Reactome database. 123 

The input layer consists of screened genes with dependency scores, and the hidden layers represent 124 

pathways. The edges between the input layer and the first hidden layer capture pathway 125 

membership for each gene, while the edges between pathway layers represent the hierarchical 126 

relationships between pathway terms. Each pathway layer is followed by a predictive layer 127 

designed to predict the mutation status of a candidate driver gene. Predictions from all hidden 128 

layers are aggregated using a voting mechanism. 129 

The model fitness of xNNDriver reflects the strength of association between a candidate gene’s 130 

mutation status and genome-wide dependency patterns. A high driver potential score therefore 131 

suggests that the gene’s mutation status is linked to a broad functional dependency signature in 132 

cancer cells and prioritizes it for driver-focused interpretation. Important pathways contributing to 133 

predictions are identified by contrasting the activation values of neural networks between mutated 134 

and non-mutated cell lines. We iteratively apply the xNNDriver model to all candidate genes 135 

(3,008 genes) to prioritize cancer driver genes and their associated important pathways. 136

## overall driver mutation status of a cancer cell line 262 

To evaluate the effectiveness of the guided learning in xAEDriver, we compared the mutation 263 

distribution of known driver SNPs with that of DVRs across all cell lines (S2 Fig). The mutation 264 

distribution of DVRs closely matched that of actual driver mutations, as demonstrated by the Earth 265 

Mover’s Distance (EMD) of 5.4 (on a 0-1,000 scale) between their mutation patterns. EMD, a 266 

metric for assessing distribution similarity, represents the minimum effort required to transform 267 

one distribution into another (29). These results underscore the effectiveness of the regularization 268 

term in mimicking the distribution of real mutations, guiding xAEDriver to actually learn DVRs 269 

in the hidden layer. 270 

Next, we examined whether each DVR’s artificial mutation status (coded as 1 in the artificial 271 

representation vector) matched the reported cancer type of its closest real SNP, determined via 272 

Euclidean distance. The cancer type matching ratio was calculated as illustrated in Fig 3B. After 273 

excluding 481 DVRs with non-unique matched real SNPs, 61 out of 440 DVRs achieved a 274 

matching ratio ≥ 0.5 (Fig 3C), suggesting that many DVRs capture cancer-type-specific mutation 275 

patterns. Among the remaining 379 DVRs with a matching ratio below 0.5, 263 (or 69%) still had 276 

a matching ratio greater than or equal to that of their corresponding real SNP. This suggests that 277 

the associated real mutations are often observed in a cancer type different from the one reported. 278 

This could be due to several factors: the driver mutation may drive tumorigenesis in another 279 

cellular context or a different cancer type, or the mutation may emerge during cancer progression 280 

as a compensatory mechanism or an accelerating “second hit”. 281

## DVRs link multi-driver functional states to drug response 299 

Having established that xAEDriver captures meaningful driver variant representations (DVRs), 300 

we next investigated whether these DVRs are associated with functional outcomes in cancer cells, 301 

specifically their ability to reflect tissue-specific drug sensitivity patterns and provide mechanistic 302 

insights into differential drug responses. To address this, we constructed LASSO regression 303 

models to predict drug sensitivity (AUC) for each drug using the DVR binary matrix across our 304 

panel of cancer cell lines (Materials and methods). DVR-based models significantly outperformed 305 

those trained on an equivalent number of random somatic mutations in the test set. Twelve DVR 306 

models achieved a test-set 𝑅 $ > 0.05 , whereas none of the 50 random models met this threshold 307 

(complete list in S5 Table). 308 

For each of the twelve DVR-based models passing this performance threshold, we identified the 309 

ten most influential DVRs based on the absolute values of their LASSO coefficients, comparing 310 

them to a control set of ten randomly selected DVRs. The biological relevance of these top DVRs 311 

was evaluated using a tissue concordance score (Materials and methods), which quantifies the 312 

alignment between each DVR’s mutation distribution across tissues and the tissue distribution of 313 

the most drug-sensitive cell lines (lowest AUC values). These top DVRs showed significantly 314 

higher tissue concordance scores than random controls, indicating that they capture tissue-specific 315 

drug response patterns (p = 0.0005, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Fig 5A). Repeating the 316 

random sampling ten times produced a consistent average p-value of 0.0015. These results 317 

demonstrate that DVRs effectively prioritize biologically meaningful, tissue-specific features 318 

associated with drug sensitivity. 319

# Discussion 367 

In this study, we introduce interpretable functional-genomics frameworks for linking cancer driver 368 

alterations to genome-wide dependency landscapes, pathway programs, and drug-response 369 

structure in cancer cell lines. xNNDriver prioritizes candidate driver genes by quantifying how 370 

strongly their mutation status is reflected in dependency profiles, while xAEDriver learns multi-371 

driver functional representations that summarize broader oncogenic states. Together, these models 372 

show that DepMap dependency data encode biologically interpretable signals of driver activity 373 

and treatment-relevant vulnerability. 374

The DepMap database 422 

We utilized data from the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) projects (23Q2 version). This 423 

release contains data from CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screens across multiple tumor types, as part of 424 

Broad’s Achilles (38) and Sanger’s SCORE projects (39). It also includes genomic and 425 

transcriptomic characterizations from the CCLE project (40). 426 

The primary dataset includes CRISPR-Cas9 essentiality screens for 17,931 genes across 1,095 cell 427 

lines (9, 10), resulting in a cell-line-by-screened-gene matrix. Each entry represents the 428 

“dependency score” of a screened gene, which was processed from the log-fold change in sgRNA 429 

read counts before and after knockout. A lower dependency score indicates a stronger reduction in 430 

cell viability following gene knockout, implying that the gene is essential for cell survival. Quality 431 

control and normalization were performed by DepMap. 432 

Somatic point mutations and indels for the DepMap cell lines were obtained from the same source 433 

(41), totalling 1,408,099 mutations, including 1,290,926 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). 434 

Gene expression data (RNA-seq) were also retrieved as transcripts per million (TPM) for 19,194 435 

genes across 1,450 cell lines, log-transformed with a pseudo-count of one. A total of 1,019 cell 436 

lines had both dependency score and gene expression data available. 437

viability values were calculated using the R package gdscIC50 (43), and standard four-parameter 439 

monotonic log-logistic dose-response curves were fitted using the R package dr4pl (44). Drug 440 

sensitivity was then quantified as the area under the curve (AUC), covering 320 drugs across 962 441

Autoencoder construction 529 

The autoencoder consists of a fully connected encoder and a biologically informed decoder. The 530 

encoder and decoder share a symmetric structure in terms of node numbers at each layer, and both 531 

use the tanh activation function. However, in the decoder, certain neuron connections are masked 532 

according to the pathway-gene mask matrix, while the encoder remains fully connected. 533 

The pathway-gene mask matrix was constructed following the approach outlined in the description 534 

for xNNDriver. The key distinction was in how we handled genes that appeared in both the 535 

dependency score and expression datasets. For such genes, the pathway was linked to both the 536 

“dependency” node and the “expression” node, and both entries in the mask matrix were set to 537 

one. For genes present in only one dataset, connections were handled as described before. 538
