# Materials and Methods 138 

Our methodology consists of four main steps. First (Fig. 1b), individual cells are segmented from 139 

tissue images, with nuclei and membranes explicitly identified. Second (Fig. 1c), each segmented 140 

cell is transformed into a low -dimensional feature vector using a contrastive learning -based 141 

approach, and this vector is enriched with cell morphology features . Third and fourth (Figs. 1d 142 

and e, respectively), each cell is associated with a phenotypic identity and assigned to a spatial 143 

neighborhood, respectively. Cell types are defined by marker expressions and morphologies, 144 

whereas neighborhoods are determined based on the spatial interactions between cells, 145 

incorporating both their phenotypic profiles and spatial contexts. 146

# Cell Type Classification (Fig. 1d) 268 

The embeddings resulting from CellMap captured both the morphological and marke r269 

expression features of each cell. We applied the Leiden clustering algorithm [31] to group cells 270 

into cellular categories in an unsupervised manner (Fig. 1d). The number of clusters was 271 

automatically determined on the basis of a user -defined resolution parameter, which controlled 272 

the coarseness of the clustering process and was the onl y required key input. This parameter 273 

can be adjusted depending on the biological question at hand, considering that higher 274 

resolutions will return more detailed subgroups, while lower values will produce coarser 275 

categories. Here, we empirically selected a resolution that reflected the well -characterized tonsil 276 

architecture to validate our method. 277 

To reduce the imposed computational load, clustering was conducted in two stages. First, we 278 

randomly selected a subset of the cell embedding vectors and used them to define the clusters 279 

(i.e., the cell types) through the Leiden algorithm. Afterward, each remaining cell was assigned 280 

to the most frequently represented cell type among its nearest labeled cells in the embedding 281 

space. Note that the cell types were defin ed based on feature vectors derived from different 282 

images, ensuring consistent cell types between the subjects and enabling the implementation 283 

of comparative analyses. 284

# via Graph Neural Networks (Fig. 1e) 287

To capture the spatial organization schemes of cells within tissues, we developed TissueNet, a 288 

self -supervised graph neural network (GNN) framework that learns context -aware cell 289 

representations to encode local tissue architectures. TissueNet id entifies cellular 290 

neighborhoods —spatial tissue regions that are characterized by specific combinations of cell 291 

types. Unlike cell -centric approaches that rely solely on physical proximity or fixed cellular 292 

densities [32,33] , TissueNet dynamically identifies n eighborhoods without requiring prior 293 

assumptions about their sizes or densities. The GNN operates over all cells within the tissue, 294 

integrating both cellular features and adjacency relationships to delineate meaningful 295 

neighborhoods, enabling a more flexib le and generalizable analysis of tissue organization 296 

schemes. 297 

To model the spatial relationships among cells, TissueNet builds an adjacency graph 298 

representing the tissue organization layout. This graph is defined by an adjacency matrix, where 299 

each cell is connected to its spatially closest neighboring cells if their center -to -center distance 300 

is at most twice the equivalent diameter target cell, which is estimated as the largest extent of 301 

the object along its principal axes using principal component analysis . The graph is denoted as 302 

𝐺 = (𝑉 , 𝐸 ), where 𝑉 represents the vertices (cells) containing the embeddings of each image, 303 

and 𝐸 ∈ 0,1𝑀𝑥𝑀 represents the edges, which define the connectivity between vertices. Here, 𝑀 304 

denotes the total number of cells contai ned in the tissue, which corresponds to the number of 305 

vertices in the graph. To reduce the memory requirements associated with storing full adjacency 306 

matrices, we represented the graph using a sparse adjacency matrix, storing only the nonzero 307 

edges in the edge list. 308 

Our GNN leverages both cell embeddings and the associated spatial structure to update each 309 

cell representation in a more context -aware and discriminative manner by identifying the most 310

# Annotated IMC dataset [19] 561 

We benchmarked UMITIC against UTAG [16] , a state -of -the -art reference method for the 562 

unsupervised discovery of tissue architectures with graphs, using a publicly available, expert -563 

annotated IMC dataset of lung samples [19] . Since the dataset provides only spatial 564 

neighborhood labels and UTAG cannot classify individual cell types, we specifically compared 565 

the spatial neighborhoods inferred by both UMITIC (TissueNet module) and UTAG against the 566 

available ex pert annotations (Fig. 7). 567 

For this purpose, TissueNet was provided the single -cell spatial coordinates and marker intensity 568 

profiles that were available in the dataset. The learned spatial -aware embeddings were then 569 

clustered using the Leiden algorithm (w ith a resolution of 0.3) and produced distinct cellular 570 

neighborhoods (Fig. 7a), which were subsequently annotated on the basis of their marker 571 

expression profiles (Fig. 7c). UTAG was independently applied to the same dataset under 572 

identical clustering set tings, allowing for a direct comparison between the UMITIC -derived 573 

neighborhoods and UTAG -defined domains. 574 

The neighborhood inference performance of the tested methods was quantified using the Rand 575 

index, which measures the degree of label concordance betw een the obtained neighborhoods 576 

and the expert annotations, and the homogeneity score, which evaluates how uniquely the 577 

predicted clusters correspond to expert annotations. Both are reference metrics for measuring 578 

accuracy in tissue domain characterization tasks and were reported in the original UTAG paper. 579 

UMITIC significantly outperformed UTAG in terms of both metrics, with homogeneity scores of 580

Despite these strengths, the limitations of our approach should also be considered. First, the 674 

accuracy of downstream analyses depends on the quality of the initial cell segmentation re sults, 675 

and errors at this stage may propagate through the pipeline. However, CellCut is based on a 676 

dual -segmentation strategy that takes advantage of two state -of -the -art cell segmentation 677 

reference methods, postprocessing their segmentations to provide a final, accurate, paired 678 

nucleus‒cytoplasm single -cell delineation procedure. Moreover, we fine -tuned and tested the 679 

nuclear segmentation approach on an in -house database, achieving high segmentation accuracy 680 

with an F1 score of 0.89. Second, the identifica tion of cell types and spatial neighborhoods relies 681 

on clustering, which introduces sensitivity to the Leiden clustering resolution parameter. While 682 

this provides flexibility, the resolution must be selected according to the desired level of 683 

biological det ail, as higher values produce finer -grained structures and lower values yield 684 

broader groupings, thereby directly affecting the granularity of the obtained results. Third, the 685 

low availability of publicly accessible hyperplex datasets limits the quantitati ve evaluation of the 686 

pipeline, particularly in hyperplex datasets with more than 20 biomarkers per sample. 687
