Abstract 26 

Spatial multi-omics holds great promise for dissecting complex biological processes, though inherent 27 technical constraints continue to limit its widespread adoption. Currently, most studies therefore 28 measure distinct omics features on separate tissue sections, necessitating spatial diagonal integration. 29 An emerging practical solution is to leverage hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) images as an integration 30 anchor, given their ubiquity, low cost, and compatibility across tissue preparations. However, this 31 anchor is frequently compromised in real-world settings by variations in H&E staining style, absence 32 of reliable histological landmarks, and mismatches in spatial resolutions across omics modalities. To 33 address this, we introduce SpaWeaver, a computational framework that couples a pathology foundation 34 model with a graph Transformer and a latent feature aligner module, providing a highly robust solution 35 for weakly anchored spatial omics data diagonal integration. Extensive experiments demonstrate that 36 SpaWeaver exhibits superior robustness against isolated or synergistic weak-anchoring factors. The 37 spatial multi-omics profiles generated by SpaWeaver link molecular features originally separated on 38 two sections, unlocking diverse downstream analyses once exclusive to co-assayed spatial multi-omics 39 data, including niche-aware cell–cell communication inference and multi-omics resolved cell state. In 40 this study, it unveils tumor-distance-dependent fibroblast–CD4⁺ T-cell signaling in human colon 41 adenocarcinoma and identifies a hypoxic glycolytic tumor state with pyknotic nuclei in human ovarian 42 cancer. Overall, our approach bridges readily accessible single-omics measurements across weakly 43 anchored tissue sections, enabling unified spatial multi-omics characterization and system-level tissue 44 analysis. 45 46

1. Introduction 47 

Biological processes are orchestrated across multiple molecular layers, such as chromatin accessibility, 48 transcription, and protein abundance, with each omics modality offering distinct biological insights 1.49 Emerging spatially resolved multi-omics sequencing technologies have been developed to profile 50 complementary molecular modalities in the spatial context 2-4 , enabling the interrogation of regulatory 51 and functional programs within complex tissues. 52 Performing multiple assays on the same tissue section typically necessitates delicate experimental 53 workflows and stringent tissue preservation conditions. Critically, each new omics combination 54 demands a bespoke experimental protocol, limiting flexibility and scalability. These challenges have 55 spurred innovation along two complementary and mutually reinforcing tracks: continued experimental 56 development of integrated spatial multi-omics assays, and computational strategies that reconstruct 57 multi-omics landscapes from separately measured data 5-7 . Notably, spatial single-omics have already 58 reached considerable maturity, offering high sensitivity, well-established protocols, and broad 59 laboratory accessibility. Building on this foundation, a compelling computational paradigm has 60 emerged: diagonal spatial omics integration, where adjacent tissue sections are profiled via distinct 61 modalities and subsequently unified in silico 8-10 . This approach inherits the sensitivity and accessibility 62 of mature single-omics platforms while remaining flexible enough to bridge any combination of 63 modalities without a dedicated co-assay protocol. However, this strategy raises a fundamental 64 challenge: how to reliably anchor and align omics features across sections that share no directly 65 measured molecular features. 66 Computational integration has emerged as a key strategy to reconstruct a comprehensive multi-omics 67 landscape from diagonally measured data. A straightforward strategy is to establish explicit links 68 between molecular features across modalities using prior biological knowledge. Exemplifying this 69 approach, SWITCH 11 integrates spatial transcriptomics (ST) and chromatin accessibility by 70 constructing peak-gene links based on genomic proximity 12 . The effectiveness of this strategy, however, 71 is inherently constrained by the completeness and tissue-specificity of available prior knowledge, as 72 the underlying regulatory landscape varies considerably across biological contexts and may not be 73 fully captured by proximity-based rules alone. Recently, a growing body of work has demonstrated 74 that unmeasured omics layers can be inferred directly from H&E images, with the learned associations 75 being context-specific and data-driven rather than dependent on static cross-omics prior knowledge. 76 This trajectory highlights histological features as a versatile and scalable anchor for spatial omics 77 integration. Building on this momentum, SpatialEx+ 9 demonstrated the viability of extending this idea 78 to spatial diagonal integration, using histological features as a common anchor to align omics 79 measurements across distinct tissue sections. 80

Yet, relying on histology as a universal bridge inherently introduces unique vulnerabilities. In real-81 world settings, this histology anchor is frequently weakened by three practical factors: H&E style 82 variation, missing histology anchors 13 , and mismatched spatial resolutions 14 . First, discrepancy in 83 tissue pretreatment, dye composition, staining duration, scanner brightness, and imaging settings can 84 conspire to induce severe domain shifts in H&E styles 15 , thereby rendering computational models 85 highly susceptible to capturing these site-specific signatures. Second, many spatial omics platforms 86 exhibit a bidirectional incompatibility with the standard H&E workflow. Specifically, the latter may 87 induce biochemical alterations in tissue, compromising downstream molecular measurements; 88 conversely, imaging-based spatial omics workflows may introduce chemical damage during repeated 89 imaging cycles and mechanical damage due to the final removal of flow-cell 16 . Third, these challenges 90 are further exacerbated by the inherent mismatch in spatial resolutions across spatial omics protocols 91 (e.g., spot-level barcode-based ST 17 versus single-cell-level spatial proteomics 18 ). Together, we refer 92 to settings in which one or more of these factors are present as weakly anchored spatial omics 93 scenarios. These weakly anchored factors undermine the fundamental premise of H&E-anchored 94 spatial omics generation methods, making existing computational models prone to capturing technical 95 confounding rather than genuine biological variations. 96 In this study, we propose SpaWeaver , a unified framework for robust integration of weakly anchored 97 spatial multi-omics data. SpaWeaver couples a pathology foundation model 19 with a graph 98 Transformer 20 to encode cellular morphology together with the intrinsic spatial context, and 99 incorporates a latent feature alignment strategy that integrates distinct tissue sections despite the severe 100 domain shifts 21 induced by weak anchoring. In a comprehensive benchmark on simulated datasets, 101 SpaWeaver substantially surpassed the existing H&E-anchored spatial omics generation methods. 102 Across four representative weakly anchored scenarios in real world, defined by two histology-anchor 103 challenges (H&E style variation and missing histology anchors), crossed with matched and 104 mismatched resolutions 22 , SpaWeaver achieved robust diagonal integration while preserving biological 105 signals, facilitating downstream analysis. Leveraging the comprehensive multi-omics landscapes 106 reconstructed by SpaWeaver, we identified tumor-distance-dependent communications between 107 fibroblasts and CD4⁺ T cells in the human colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) microenvironment. In 108 human ovarian cancer (OV) tissue, SpaWeaver uncovered a spatial domain enriched for an epithelial 109 subpopulation characterized by hypoxic glycolytic programs and pyknotic nuclei, together with a lipid-110 metabolism-associated macrophages enriched domain that were frequently located nearby. 111

2. Results 112 

Overall architecture of SpaWeaver 113 

SpaWeaver is designed for robust spatial diagonal integration (Fig. 1a) under diverse weakly anchored 114 scenarios. In many spatial multi-omics studies, different molecular modalities are measured on 115 adjacent tissue sections rather than on the same section, owing to the limited flexibility and availability 116 of current co-assay technologies. From a computational perspective, this design produces a diagonal-117 like data structure: each section contains measurements for only a subset of molecular features. Spatial 118 diagonal integration aims to complete this incomplete matrix by inferring the missing molecular 119 profiles across sections, thereby generating in silico co-assayed spatial multi-omics data. 120 A common strategy is to use H&E images from adjacent sections as a shared anchor. Existing H&E-121 anchored spatial omics generation methods 23,24 typically learn mappings from histological features to 122 omics profiles within each section and then apply these mappings across sections to infer the 123 unmeasured modalities. Some methods, such as SpatialEx+, further regularize the training of modality-124 specific predictors with additional alignment objectives. However, these strategies build on a reliable 125 histology anchor across sections. In practice, this assumption is often weakened by H&E style variation, 126 missing histology anchors, and mismatched spatial resolutions across spatial omics platforms (Fig. 1b). 127 We refer to these settings as weakly anchored scenarios. 128 SpaWeaver addresses these challenges through a sequential architecture, it first extracts high-129 dimensional morphological representations from cell-, bin-, or spot-centered patches using a pre-130 trained H&E foundation model. These feature representations are then fed into a graph Transformer 131 module equipped with a latent feature aligner (Fig. 1c). Conventional approaches typically rely on 132 local message-passing mechanisms to capture neighborhood context 25,26 , but such strategies are 133 constrained in modeling broader, tissue-level microenvironmental factors and long-range intercellular 134 interactions that may shape cellular states beyond the immediate neighborhood 27 . To transcend these 135 limitations, SpaWeaver adopts a graph Transformer architecture that enables multi-scale spatial 136 context modeling (Fig. 1d, see Methods “Graph Transformer module”), conceptualizing cells 137 analogously to tokens in natural language 28 . Specifically, a neighborhood tokenization strategy is 138 employed to construct feature sequences for each cell, then neighborhood-aware message passing is 139 subsequently leveraged to capture multi-scale spatial dependencies. 140 Next, to mitigate the domain shifts in weakly anchored data, these spatially aware histological 141 representations are further regularized by minimizing the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) 29 

142 between sections in reproducing kernel Hilbert space 30 (Fig. 1e, see Methods “Latent feature aligner”). 143 By doing so, cells with a similar biological context across sections are mapped close together in the 144

Comprehensive benchmarking of panel diagonal integration under H&E style variation 152 

To evaluate SpaWeaver’s resilience to H&E image style variation during the panel diagonal integration, 153 we established a benchmark using a human breast cancer dataset profiled by Xenium 31 (Fig. 2a, top). 154 This dataset measured the same 313-gene panel on two serial sections, both paired with H&E staining, 155 providing a well-controlled setting for quantitative evaluation. Specifically, we first split the measured 156 gene panel into two non-overlapping subsets, assigning panel A (150 genes) to slice 1 and panel B (163 157 genes) to slice 2, while withholding the remaining genes in each slice to serve as the ground truth for 158 quantitative evaluation (Fig. 2a, bottom). Originally, the two H&E images were generated in the same 159 staining batch, they showed similar staining style and image quality (Supplementary Fig. 1). We then 160 applied a staining perturbation algorithm 32 on the H&E image from slice 2 (Fig. 2b, top) to simulate a 161 weakly anchored scenario with H&E style variation. Uniform manifold approximation and projection 162 (UMAP) visualization 33 , quantitative evaluation via scIB 34 metrics, and the recently proposed 163 scGraph 35 (see Methods “Evaluation”) consistently demonstrated that this synthetic perturbation 164 introduced a pronounced domain shift in cellular histological representations produced by the H&E 165 foundation model (Fig. 2b, bottom). 166 We first evaluated whether this stylistic domain shift distorted the predicted molecular profiles by 167 comparing SpaWeaver with 8 methods supporting histology anchored spatial diagonal integration 168 (SpatialEx+ 9, DeepPT 36 , iStar 37 , sCellST 24 , MISO 38 , HiST 39 , THItoGene 40 , and Hist2ST 41 ). Quantitative 169 comparison demonstrated that SpaWeaver consistently outperforms competing methods in both batch-170 effect removal and biological conservation (Fig. 2c, top, and Supplementary Table 1). Notably, 171 regarding iLISI, most baseline methods showed values close to zero, whereas SpaWeaver reached 0.8. 172 Regarding biological conservation, several baselines underperformed relative to the original cellular 173 histological representations; in sharp contrast, SpaWeaver increased the K-NMI score by more than 174 twofold. This indicates that our framework effectively attenuates staining-associated domain shifts 175 without damaging intrinsic biological structure. 176 UMAP visualizations further corroborate these findings: expression profiles predicted by DeepPT and 177 SpatialEx+ exhibited pronounced section-specific segregation, whereas SpaWeaver’s predictions were 178 seamlessly blended (Fig. 2c, middle, and Supplementary Fig. 2a). SpaWeaver achieved a significantly lower GTE 181 compared to DeepPT and SpatialEx+ (Fig. 2c, bottom, and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Challenge of spatial multi-omics diagonal integration without histology anchor 272 

In spatial multi-omics studies, proteomics provides essential protein-level information that enables a 273 more direct characterization of cell identities, functional states, and tissue microenvironment 274 organization beyond transcriptomics alone. For example, tumor immunology studies often profile ST 275 on one tissue section and spatial proteomics on an adjacent section to jointly characterize immune cell 276 states and tumor-immune interactions. However, spatial proteomics datasets are commonly generated 277 without a matched H&E image, because antibody-based staining, iterative imaging-stripping 278 workflows are optimized for protein detection and may compromise tissue morphology (Fig. 4a). This 279 phenomenon is widespread across representative benchmarks from SPATCH 47 and STP-Portal 13 ,280 spanning diverse tissue types and multiplexed spatial proteomics platforms such as CODEX 18 and 281 IBEX 48 (Fig. 4b). Although adjacent transcriptomic and proteomic sections provide a nominal pair, the 282 absence of a shared histology anchor, compounded by non-overlapping molecular features, makes this 283 correspondence intrinsically weak. This challenging diagonal integration setting remains beyond the 284 scope of existing methods. 285 Notably, the multiplexed spatial proteomics channels contain native, morphology-related signals that 286 inherently resemble key components of classical H&E staining. Specifically, DAPI nuclear staining, 287 routinely acquired for single-cell segmentation, captured explicit nuclear morphology, while 288 cytoplasmic and membrane protein markers outline coarse cellular contours. These intrinsic biological 289 signals can therefore be leveraged to synthesize virtual H&E images 14 . As an illustrative example, we 290 utilized a COAD tissue section profiled by CODEX from the SPATCH dataset to generate virtual H&E 291 images; however, the raw synthetic outputs showed substantial style variations compared with the real 292 H&E images acquired from the adjacent slice (Fig. 4c, center). Given that image style transfer is well-293 established in computer vision and CycleGAN 49 has been widely used for unsupervised image-to-294 image translation without requiring paired training data, we applied CycleGAN to align the virtual 295 H&E images into the exact visual style of the real H&E, a process we referred to as virtual H&E 296 rectification, yielding “rectified H&E” (Fig. 4c, right). Remarkably, the resulting rectified H&E images 297 closely resembled the true experimental H&E references upon visual inspection, preserving both 298 macroscopic tissue architecture and finer morphological details (Fig. 4d). 299 We next extracted cellular histological representations by passing cellular patches from the real, virtual, 300 and rectified H&E images through the H&E foundation model, enabling a direct comparison of their 301 feature spaces before and after style rectification (Fig. 4e). Strikingly, UMAP visualization revealed 302 that despite visual similarities, the cellular representations from both the virtual and rectified H&E 303 images exhibit a pronounced, systematic separation from those generated by the real H&E patches 304 (Fig. 4e). These empirical findings demonstrated that while CycleGAN succeeded in optimizing 305 macro-level style stylization, it introduced subtle, watermark-like artifacts that, although imperceptible 306

Spatial multi-omics diagonal integration without histology anchor 322 

Given that spatial proteomics datasets commonly lack co-stained H&E images, we next considered a 323 weakly anchored scenario without histology anchor (Fig. 5a). The same COAD dataset in the previous 324 section was adopted for this benchmark. Specifically, the dataset comprises two serial slices, with slice 325 1 being profiled by Xenium and paired with a co-stained H&E image, whereas slice 2 being profiled 326 by CODEX but lacked a co-stained H&E image. Building upon the insights in the previous section, 327 SpaWeaver generated a virtual H&E image for slice 2 as the surrogate anchor. During model 328 optimization, SpaWeaver flexibly incorporated prior information when available, and the readily 329 accessible cell type annotations were utilized in this scenario (Fig. 5b). For the ST slice, these 330 annotations can be derived using established ST annotation methods, such as Spatial-ID 50 . Conversely, 331 considering that spatial proteomics panels typically profile only dozens of markers, we further 332 developed a dedicated marker-based annotation strategy tailored specifically for spatial proteomics 333 data (see Methods “Data”). 3. Discussion 436 

A foundational objective of spatial multi-omics is to realize a unified, multi-dimension molecular 437 characterization of complex tissues by concurrently resolving disparate omics layers within their native 438 architectural contexts. However, despite the exponential diversification of spatial profiling 439 technologies, the simultaneous experimental measurement of distinct molecular modalities on a single 440 tissue section remains technically prohibitive, financially taxing, and inherently difficult to scale. 441 Consequently, a vast majority of translational spatial studies partition different molecular layers across 442 adjacent tissue sections, creating a critical open challenge for spatial diagonal integration. 443 Existing computational approaches often heavily rely on identical biological prior or shared H&E 444 histology anchors to guide this integration. In real-world multi-omics experiments, however, this strict 445 assumption is frequently violated: H&E images may exhibit substantial style variability, histology 446 anchors may be entirely missing for certain modalities, and spatial resolutions may differ markedly 447 across diverse omics assays. To render spatial diagonal integration broadly applicable in such realistic 448 experimental settings, a dedicated framework capable of explicitly accommodating these weakly 449 anchored spatial omics data is critically required. To bridge this technological chasm, we developed 450 SpaWeaver, an AI-driven computational framework for the robust integration of weakly anchored 451 spatial multi-omics. In rigorous benchmarking simulations mimicking severe H&E style variation, 452 SpaWeaver outperformed eight state-of-the-art alternative algorithms by a substantial margin. 453 Furthermore, across extensive evaluations in more complex, real-world scenarios, SpaWeaver 454 maintained exceptional robustness even when multiple weak-anchoring challenges were intertwined. 455 However, several limitations of the current framework warrant acknowledgment. First, due to the 456 practical bottleneck in acquiring benchmarks with three or more omics layers, SpaWeaver was not 457 explicitly optimized for multi-section diagonal integration involving arbitrary tissue slices. We 458 anticipate that the emergence of such multi-layered datasets will naturally catalyze the next generation 459 of computational methods for these unconstrained scenarios, ultimately enabling a more holistic, pan-460 tissue-scale multi-omics synthesis. Second, although our evaluation revealed that image-generating 461 models like CycleGAN may introduce subtle computational domain shifts, cross-modal image 462 translation remains a promising avenue for future exploration, particularly for imaging-based spatial 463 assays. 464 Despite these open avenues for optimization, SpaWeaver substantially broadens the computational 465 horizons of spatial diagonal integration and democratizes the utility of experimentally accessible, 466 single-modality spatial omics datasets. By reconstructing high-resolution spatial multi-omics within a 467 single tissue slice in silico, SpaWeaver establishes a rich spatial molecular context for diverse 468 downstream mechanistic discoveries. The SpaWeaver model 473 

SpaWeaver is a novel graph-based framework designed for the robust integration of weakly anchored 474 spatial omics data. We denoted the molecular feature expression measured on the two adjacent tissue 475 sections as 𝑋 ! ∈ ℝ"!×$! and 𝑋 % ∈ ℝ""×$" , respectively, where the molecular sets are non-476 overlapping across the two sections. Here, the constant 𝑁 ! and 𝑁 % denote the numbers of cells 477 captured within the respective sections, whereas the constant 𝐹 ! and 𝐹 % denote the numbers of 478 profiled molecular features in 𝑋 ! and 𝑋 % , respectively. Throughout the following methodological 479 formulations, we utilized 𝑋 as a unified notation when no explicit distinction was required of the two 480 sections, indicating that the corresponding computational operation was applied to both sections. This 481 identical convention was consistently extended to other algorithmic variables and parameters unless 482 stated otherwise. 483 The architecture of SpaWeaver (Fig. 1c) comprises four major components: (1) an H&E foundation 484 model, (2) a graph Transformer module, (3) a latent feature aligner, and (4) dual omics predictors. 485 Within each individual tissue slice, SpaWeaver first deploys an H&E foundation model to extract 486 morphologically informative features from either real or virtual histology images. Then, a graph 487 Transformer module integrates multi-scale spatial context across the spatial cell graph to generate 488 spatially aware cellular morphological embeddings. To accommodate weakly anchored data, the latent 489 feature aligner is specifically designed to mitigate domain shifts in the learned representations. Finally, 490 each omics-specific predictor infers the corresponding omics profiles from the encoded cellular 491 embeddings. Together, these components enable SpaWeaver to establish a direct, end-to-end 492 computational bridge between tissue morphologies and molecular profiles across different omics. 493 Detailed mathematical and algorithmic formulations of each constitutive module are provided in the 494 subsequent sections. 495

Data 676 

Spatial transcriptome data. We applied platform-specific quality control procedures for different ST 677 technologies, followed by a unified preprocessing pipeline. All these steps were implemented using 678 the Scanpy 73 package. 679 

Quality control. For Xenium datasets, cells with fewer than 50 total gene expression counts were 680 removed. For Visium HD datasets, analyses were performed at 8 𝜇 m resolution. Bins with fewer than 681 50 total gene expression counts were filtered out, and bins with more than 20% mitochondrial gene 682 expression were excluded. 683 

Preprocessing. Raw gene expression counts were normalized using scanpy.pp.normalize_total 684 followed by log-transformation with scanpy.pp.log1p . For Visium, Visium HD, Xenium 5K datasets, 685 the top 500 spatially variable genes were selected using Moran’s I statistics. For Xenium datasets, no 686 gene filtering was applied, as the targeted gene panel comprises a limited and predefined set of genes. 687 

Simulation of stain variability. To simulate staining variability across experiments, we applied 688 perturbation on H&E images. Specifically, the hematoxylin and eosin channels were independently 689 perturbed using randomly sampled multiplicative scaling factors from [1 − 𝛼, 1 + 𝛼] and additive 690 offsets from [−𝛽, 𝛽] , with 𝛼 = 𝛽 = 0.2 . The D (background) channel was not perturbed. This 691 perturbation introduces moderate staining variation while preserving underlying tissue morphology. 692 

Spatial proteomics data. 693 

Preprocessing. For spatial proteomics data, protein intensities were normalized using a per-cell 694 geometric mean scaling and subsequently log-transformed, as commonly used in prior studies 74 .695 

Virtual H&E generation. To overcome the lack of H&E images, we generated virtual RGB images 696 using the TissueTag package 14 , which implements a method adapted from Simonson et al. 75 to construct 697 virtual H&E images from two user-defined fluorescent channels. 698 

Annotation. For each cell, marker-based scores were calculated for each candidate cell type using 699 curated marker proteins. Cells were then ranked according to their scores for each cell type, and labels 700 were assigned by selecting the highest-scoring cells until the predefined proportion for that cell type 701 was reached. H&E image denoising. 704 

We observed periodic stripe-like artifacts in cell-level H&E embeddings from several low-quality 705 H&E images, including the COAD and OV samples in the SPATCH dataset, despite these artifacts 706 being barely visible in the raw images. To reduce their effect on model training, we applied sinusoidal 707 filtering to each embedding dimension along the spatial x and y axes. Barcode-based data used the 708 original barcode coordinates, whereas cell-resolution data were spatially binned before frequency-709 domain artifact detection and sinusoidal component removal. For the OV dataset, an additional 710 autoencoder-based denoising step was applied to remove residual nonlinear noise. The source code is 711 publicly available in the SpaWeaver package. 712

Spatial domain identification. In this study, spatial domain identification is performed in two distinct 736 contexts, both implemented using CellCharter 44 , but serving different purposes. In the quantitative 737 evaluation, spatial domain identification is used to indirectly assess whether the predicted gene 738 expression preserves the underlying biological structure. Specifically, we applied CellCharter 739 independently to the observed gene expression and to the predicted gene expression. The spatial 740 domains derived from the observed gene expression are treated as the ground truth, and the agreement 741 between the two domain partitions is quantified using the ARI. In the qualitative analysis, we jointly 742 input the gene expression profiles predicted by each method across the two tissue sections into 743 CellCharter for integrated spatial domain identification. In addition, we attempted to mitigate domain-744 shift effects in affected methods using scVI-based batch correction 45 , directly relying on the scVI 745 implementation provided in the CellCharter package. 746 

Usage of scIB package 34 . We evaluated batch effect removal performance using established metrics 747 implemented in scIB, including batch correction metrics (iLISI, S-batch, kBET, ASW, and graph 748 connectivity) and biological conservation metrics (cLISI, S-label, and kNMI). All metrics were 749 computed on the concatenated gene expression profiles of the two predicted panels from both slices, 750 followed by dimensionality reduction to the top 50 principal components. For computational efficiency, 751 we down-sampled the data to a maximum of 100,000 cells before metric computation. The clustering 752 step for kNMI was implemented using K-Means, whereas all other metrics were computed using the 753 original implementations provided in the scIB package. 754 

scGraph 35 is used to evaluate whether the gene expression profiles predicted by a given algorithm 755 preserve the underlying biological structure of the original data. Specifically, cell-type centroids are 756 computed within each batch using trimmed means in PCA space, and pairwise distances between 757 centroids are used to construct cell-type relational graphs. The consistency between graphs derived 758 from predicted representations and a batch-wise consensus graph is then quantified using distance-759 weighted correlation metrics. 760
