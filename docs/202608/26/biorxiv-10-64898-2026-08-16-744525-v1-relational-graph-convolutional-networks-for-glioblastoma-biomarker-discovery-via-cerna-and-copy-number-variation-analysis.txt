ABSTRACT 2

Glioblastoma (GBM) is a highly aggressive brain tumor with an extremely poor five-year 3

survival rate of 6.9%, largely attributable to the lack of reliable biomarkers. While competing 4

endogenous RNA (ceRNA) and copy number variation (CNV) analyses each offer unique 5

biomarker-identification potential, current approaches neglect the integration of multiple regulatory 6

mechanisms for biomarker detection. To address this limitation, we applied relational graph 7

convolutional networks (RGCNs) to ceRNA and CNV knowledge graphs through a novel late-8

fusion ensemble architecture. The proposed architecture outperformed baseline models and 9

identified five novel biomarkers, including hsa-miR-196a and hsa-miR-224. Kaplan–Meier survival 10 

analysis and Cox regression indicated that the identified genes hold significant prognostic and 11 

diagnostic power, and the early stratification of the Kaplan–Meier curves indicates their potential 12 

for patient survival prediction. The results illustrate that a late-fusion RGCN ensemble effectively 13 

captures complex gene interactions, overcoming limitations of existing models and providing a 14 

framework for future biomarker discovery. The novel biomarkers serve as prospective targets for 15 

future GBM therapeutic development and candidates for non-invasive diagnostic assays. 16

1 INTRODUCTION 

Brain cancer is a highly pervasive disease that poses a significant global health concern, with over 246,000 19 

recorded deaths (Ostrom et al., 2023). Among brain cancers, glioblastoma is the most lethal and common 20 

form, accounting for over half of all cases in adults, with a 6.9% five-year survival rate (Ostrom et al., 21 

2023). This outcome is largely driven by the lack of reliable biomarkers. This prevents accurate patient 22 

subtyping and diagnosis, forcing clinicians to rely on the overly generalized Stupp protocol (surgical 23 

resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy) regardless of tumor responsiveness (Roncevic et al., 2025). 24 

Identifying biomarkers in glioblastoma remains a challenge, primarily due to the complex nature of 25 

gene regulation. Current statistical approaches fail to capture these molecular interactions, resulting in few 26

1.1 Competing Endogenous RNA (ceRNA) Mechanisms in Glioblastoma 29 

Noncoding RNAs “talk” to each other, forming a regulatory network known as the competing endogenous 30 

RNA (ceRNA) network (Salmena et al., 2011). When microRNA (miRNA) binds to messenger RNA 31 

(mRNA), the miRNA silences the mRNA and prevents it from being translated into a protein. However, 32 

long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) and several other types of RNA compete to bind to miRNA; when they 33 

do, they “sponge” the miRNA, freeing the silenced mRNA and ensuring its translation (Figure 1). 34 

Figure 1. ceRNA theory. In the normal state, miRNA binds to mRNA and prevents translation; in the ceRNA state, lncRNA sponges the miRNA, allowing the mRNA to be translated into a protein. Recent research has demonstrated that the ceRNA complex plays a vital role in glioblastoma regulation. 35 

Su et al. (2021) confirmed the involvement of the lncRNA MALAT1 in glioblastoma development: 36 

when MALAT1 prevented the expression of miR-613, a miRNA implicated in glioblastoma, cancer 37 

cells proliferated uncontrollably. Because these dysregulated RNAs drive glioblastoma, they function as 38 

molecular biomarkers, and a comprehensive analysis of ceRNA interactions presents an opportunity to 39 

identify novel glioblastoma biomarkers. 40 

1.2 Graph Theory 41 

A graph consists of a set of points (nodes) together with lines (edges) joining certain pairs of these points, 42 

where nodes represent objects and edges denote relationships between them (Figure 2). A homogeneous 43 

graph is limited to a single node type and a single edge type. To address this limitation, this study 44 

employs heterogeneous graphs, which contain multiple node and edge types. Heterogeneous graphs are 45 

particularly well suited for this study because ceRNA networks have multiple node and edge types: two 46 

edge (interaction) types—mRNA silencing and miRNA sponging—and three node (gene) types—miRNA, 47 

mRNA, and lncRNA. To capture the broader medical context, nodes are assigned features from clinical 48

1.3 Machine Learning for ceRNA Network Analysis 51 

A Relational Graph Convolutional Network (RGCN) is part of a class of neural networks operating on 52 

graphs, developed specifically for heterogeneous graphs (Schlichtkrull et al., 2017). This architecture is 53 

equipped to process complex data by distinguishing between different types of relationships and nodes. 54 

To understand the broader structure of the graph and the context of a node, RGCNs use message passing, 55 

which builds a rich representation of a node by aggregating neighboring node data and recursively updating 56

1.4 Limitations of Current Statistical Approaches 63 

Existing studies have applied various traditional ML and statistical methods to identify biomarkers via 64 

ceRNA network analysis. Liu et al. (2021) used weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), 65 

which investigates the role of mRNA clusters by analyzing highly correlated genes. That study conducted 66 

differential expression analysis, then a Functional Enrichment Analysis (FEA) to narrow the candidates, 67 

and finally multivariate Cox regressions to evaluate each gene’s effect on survival, concluding that WGCNA 68 

could identify several genes associated with glioblastoma. However, WGCNA assumes simple, correlative 69 

relationships, which prevents it from identifying biomarkers participating in the more complex (non-linear) 70 

ceRNA interactions that characterize a significant amount of ceRNA in glioblastoma (Sumazin et al., 2011). 71 

Other approaches face similar limitations. Bazrgar et al. (2024) used a seed graph to construct a ceRNA 72 

network, applying differential expression analysis, using the most dysregulated genes as seed nodes, 73 

expanding the network using protein-protein interaction databases, and applying multivariate Cox regression 74 

to identify three prognostic biomarkers. Although this method is robust because it builds on genes known 75 

to be significant, it overly emphasizes the seed node and assumes that mRNA expression is directly related 76 

to related-protein expression. In glioblastoma, however, the correlation between mRNA expression and its 77 

related protein can be low due to post-transcriptional processing such as miRNA silencing (Sumazin et al., 78 

2011), producing statistical correlations between non-interacting genes. Deep learning architectures such 79 

as RGCNs address this using network deconvolution, which removes indirect gene interactions and leaves 80 

direct, causal ones. 81

1.5 Interpretability in Deep Learning Architectures 82 

A key challenge in adopting deep learning over statistical methods is interpretability. Rudin (2019) 83 

argues that statistical models are more suitable than black-box models for high-stakes domains due to 84 

their interpretability. While this argument holds for linear or tabular data, it is less applicable to molecular 85 

biology, because ceRNA networks are a multilayered gene regulatory system in which genes communicate 86 

through complex, non-linear interactions (Sumazin et al., 2011). RGCNs address this through their message-87 

passing and aggregation mechanism, which converts large numbers of input features into meaningful node 88 

representations. To bridge the interpretability gap, future work could employ explainer methods such as 89 

GNNExplainer (Ying et al., 2019), which quantifies the influence of input components on the output. 90 

1.6 Research Gap and Research Goals 91 

The study by Zhi et al. (2025) evaluating RGCNs for ceRNA analysis in gastric cancer is vital to 92 

understanding the research gap: RGCNs outperformed other ML models, such as random forest and logistic 93 

regression, in biomarker identification. Despite this, current analyses of glioblastoma ceRNA largely 94 

depend on statistical measures such as the WGCNA method of Liu et al. (2021). Relying on correlation-95 

based models is ineffective, as glioblastoma is characterized by extreme lineage plasticity—cancer cells 96 

transition between states depending on their environment—which causes ceRNA networks to become 97 

transient (e.g., a lncRNA may sponge a miRNA but lose that function hours later) (Neftel et al., 2019). 98 

Because these models rely on static, linear methods, they average out transient gene interactions and fail to 99 

pinpoint the true biomarkers driving glioblastoma. 100 

This paper addresses this gap by employing RGCNs to analyze the ceRNA network of glioblastoma. 101 

2 MATERIALS AND METHODS 

This study employed a quantitative deep learning framework adapted from the methodology of Zhi et al. 102 

(2025). It began by identifying the most effective model for ceRNA analysis, then validated the biomarkers 103 

identified by that model to ensure prognostic and diagnostic value. The RGCN architecture was tested 104 

against three standard ML algorithms: (1) random forest (RF), (2) multilayer perceptron (MLP), and 105 

(3) logistic regression. Standard ML validation metrics, such as the area under the receiver operating 106 

characteristic curve (AUCROC) and F1 score, were used to compare their efficacy. All analyses and data 107 

collection were performed in Python and made publicly available on GitHub to ensure reproducibility. An 108 

overview of the analysis pipeline is shown in Figure 5. 109 

2.1 Data Preparation and Processing 110 

Diseased-tissue gene expression data was obtained from the TCGA-GBM (The Cancer Genome Atlas 111 

Glioblastoma) dataset via the UCSC Xena Toil Recompute Project (The Cancer Genome Atlas Research 112 

Network, 2008; Goldman et al., 2020). TCGA-GBM contains RNA sequencing data from tumor samples 113 

for mRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA. It was selected over alternative datasets (e.g., GSE90604) due to its 114 

high sample count and comprehensive clinical metadata, though it largely contains samples from Western 115 

populations, limiting applicability. Because TCGA-GBM contains limited normal-tissue data (required 116 

for differential expression analysis), healthy control data were acquired from the GTEx dataset (The 117 

GTEx Consortium, 2013); because GTEx lacks miRNA profiles, the few normal samples provided by 118 

TCGA-GBM were used for miRNA, which likely weakened the statistical strength of some metrics. Both 119

2.1.1 Preprocessing 122 

RNA columns were checked for standard issues. Of 612 available samples, 14 duplicates, 11 low-quality, 123 

and 14 outliers were removed, leaving 573 samples. Outliers were removed using PyDESeq2 (Muzellec 124 

et al., 2023); samples were considered low-quality if they lacked clinical metadata. Missing gene expression 125 

values were set to 0, and expected count values were rounded to the nearest integer for compatibility with 126 

the Python libraries. Gene type classifications were obtained from the MyGene.info API v3 (Wu et al., 127 

2013). 128 

2.1.2 Differential Expression Analysis 129 

Differential Expression (DE) analysis identifies genes that are significantly upregulated or downregulated 130 

and quantifies fold change. This study used PyDESeq2 0.5.4 (Muzellec et al., 2023). To control the false 131 

discovery rate, a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value < 0.05 and log 2 |fold change | > 1 were used. The 132 

analysis yielded 1847 differentially expressed mRNA, 234 lncRNA, and 312 miRNA. To confirm validity, 133 

Feature Enrichment Analysis (FEA) was performed, identifying pathways that aligned with existing studies 134 

(Bazrgar et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2021). The enriched Gene Ontology biological processes and KEGG 135 

pathways are shown in Figures 6 and 7. 136

3 RESULTS 

3.1 Model Performance 137 

The RGCN model and baseline ML algorithms were trained and tested on the same processed dataset. The 138 

RGCN achieved the highest classification ability by AUCROC. Although the RF and MLP models reported 139 

higher accuracy, their output was of little value: they classified nearly every node as a non-biomarker due 140 

to the extreme class imbalance, producing AUC values close to 0.5. The RGCN reached a peak AUCROC 141 

of 0.840 (mean 0.762 ± 0.098 across 10-fold CV) with an accuracy of 0.806, substantially outperforming 142 

the baselines and showing a smaller standard deviation, indicating robustness to class imbalance. Its low 143 

precision (0.034) indicated a high false-positive rate, a critical bottleneck requiring downstream in vitro 144 

filtering. The corresponding ROC curves are shown in Figure 8. 145

3.3 Prognostic Validation 151 

Cox regression showed that four of the five candidate biomarkers had significant prognostic value ( p < 152 

0.01 ), with hazard ratios ranging from 0.56 (protective) to 1.77 (increased risk) and confidence intervals 153 

that did not cross 1.0. hsa-miR-1264 and hsa-miR-1269a were protective (HR = 0 .56 ; downregulation 154 

reduces mortality risk by 44%), while hsa-miR-130b (HR = 1 .74 ) and hsa-miR-135a-1 (HR = 1 .77 )155 

increased mortality risk by 74% and 77% respectively. hsa-miR-1248 did not show a significant survival 156 

effect and was excluded from further survival analysis. The Cox regression results are shown in Figure 9. 157 

Kaplan–Meier curves, stratified by median expression, separated early ( ∼200 days) and maintained that 158 

separation over the 2000-day TCGA window, confirming long-term predictive ability (Figure 10). 159 

For the protective biomarkers, the high-expression group had a median survival of ∼800 days versus 160 

∼400 days for the low-expression group (a doubling of survival time); for the oncogenic biomarkers, the 161 

relationship reversed (a 50% reduction). These results are summarized in Table 2. 162

4 DISCUSSION 

Glioblastoma is characterized by high mortality and significant tumor heterogeneity, and currently has few 163 

identified biomarkers, making tailored diagnosis and treatment difficult. ceRNA provides an opportunity 164 

to understand the gene regulatory system driving glioblastoma, but current methods largely depend on 165 

statistical measures that assume linear relationships and therefore misconstrue gene interactions. 166 

This study aimed to bridge the gap in glioblastoma biomarker detection using deep learning. By modeling 167 

miRNA-lncRNA-mRNA interactions as a heterogeneous graph and training an RGCN on it, the primary 168

A primary limitation is the class imbalance, which skewed the model toward non-biomarkers, lowered 176 

overall accuracy, and may have confounded results. Training data came solely from TCGA-GBM, which 177 

largely represents Western populations, potentially limiting generalizability (Verhaak et al., 2010). The 178
