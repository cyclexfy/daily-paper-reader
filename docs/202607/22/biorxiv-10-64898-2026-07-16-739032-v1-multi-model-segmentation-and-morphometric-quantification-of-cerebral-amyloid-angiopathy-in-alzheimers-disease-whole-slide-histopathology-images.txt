Abstract 18 

Introduction: Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is characterized by amyloid-beta deposition in 19 cortical and leptomeningeal vessels and associated with cognitive impairment and hemorrhage. 20 Current neuropathological assessments rely on semiquantitative grading and lack vessel-level 21 resolution and scalability. Existing computational pathology approaches also fail to capture 22 individual vessel morphology and spatial amyloid distribution across whole-slide images (WSIs). To 23 address this gap, we developed a deep learning framework for reproducible, quantitative analysis of 24 CAA in WSIs. 25 

Methods: We analyzed 20 postmortem brain tissue sections from the frontal (n = 10) and occipital 26 cortices (n = 10) of 10 individuals with Alzheimer’s disease pathology obtained from the University 27 of Pittsburgh Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center, which served as the internal development cohort. 28 An independent external cohort consisted of 10 sections (5 frontal and 5 occipital samples) from 5 29 individuals obtained from the University of Kentucky Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center. We 30 trained and compared three semantic segmentation architectures, a standard U-Net, a dual-attention 31 residual U-Net (DA-ResUNet), and a Swin Transformer-based U-Net (Swin-UNet), using the 32 internal development cohort with slide-level five-fold cross-validation. All models were evaluated on 33 the independent external cohort to assess generalization under domain shift. Based on segmentation 34 performance and computational efficiency, we selected one architecture to generate whole-slide 35 composite segmentation masks for vessel walls, amyloid deposits, and tissue compartments. These 36 masks were subsequently used for deterministic vessel detection, morphometric measurements, and 37 quantification of vascular and perivascular amyloid features through post-processing analysis. 38 

Results: All three architectures achieved high segmentation accuracy on the internal cohort, with 39 Dice scores above 90% across vessel walls, amyloid deposits, gray matter, and leptomeninges. The 40 Swin-UNet showed marginally higher performance for vessel segmentation, whereas the DA-41 ResUNet provided more balanced accuracy and computational efficiency and was selected for 42 downstream analysis. External cohort evaluation demonstrated robust generalization, with attention-43 enhanced models outperforming the standard U-Net under domain shift. Using the selected model, 44 the pipeline reliably detected valid vessels, excluded non-vascular artifacts, and enabled deterministic 45 extraction of vessel morphometry, vascular and perivascular amyloid burden, and identification of 46 circumferential CAA involvement at the vessel level. 47 

Discussion: This framework provides a scalable, interpretable solution for vessel-level CAA 48 analysis, supporting robust geometric and spatial characterization of cerebrovascular pathology and 49 enabling future integration with clinical and genetic studies. Beyond CAA, the modular design 50 allows extension to other vascular pathologies, including arteriolosclerosis, in WSIs, facilitating 51 broader investigation of cerebrovascular disease mechanisms. 52 53

1 Introduction 54 

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a cerebrovascular disorder defined by pathological amyloid-55 beta (Aβ) deposition in the wall of parenchymal and leptomeningeal blood vessels (Revesz et al., 56 2003). This vascular pathology contributes to cognitive impairment and is a frequent driver of 57 cerebral microhemorrhages and lobar hemorrhages in older adults (Rus Prelog et al., 2025). CAA 58 commonly coexists with Alzheimer’s disease neuropathologic change (ADNC), with autopsy studies 59 showing vascular Aβ in more than 80% of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) brains (Ellis et al., 1996). This 60 vascular pathology adds to the overall disease burden of AD (Ringman et al., 2014). 61 Despite its clinical and pathological importance, current semiquantitative grading systems limit 62 understanding of how vessel-level features, including Aβ burden, vessel caliber, and anatomic 63 distribution, relate to disease severity and clinical outcomes (Attems et al., 2011; Greenberg et al., 64 2020). The Vonsattel grading system scores Aβ severity at the level of individual vessels based on 65 wall involvement and structural damage, but it does not capture overall disease burden or anatomic 66 distribution (Vonsattel et al., 1991). The Olichney scoring system, by contrast, summarizes regional 67 involvement but cannot represent mixed patterns of leptomeningeal and parenchymal pathology 68 within the same specimen (Olichney et al., 1995). Both approaches are prone to interobserver 69 variability, and impractical for large-scale quantitative analysis. 70 Automated analysis of whole slide images (WSIs) using deep learning (DL) offers a path toward 71 objective, scalable, and reproducible quantification of CAA. In principle, such approaches can enable 72 vessel-level morphometric measurements and precise spatial mapping of Aβ burden across large 73 tissue sections (Timakova et al., 2023). However, existing computational methods for CAA analysis 74 remain incomplete. Prior DL frameworks quantify Aβ and vascular features using area-based or 75 density-based measures within manually defined cortical and leptomeningeal regions (Perosa et al., 76 2021). These approaches do not treat vessels as discrete structural units and thus do not support 77 vessel-level morphometric analysis or assessment of heterogeneity among vessels within the same 78 region .79 More recent computational frameworks have moved toward vessel-centered analysis by segmenting 80 individual vessels and deriving morphometric indices, such as wall thickness, in the setting of brain 81 arteriolosclerosis and renal arteriosclerosis (Jin et al., 2024; Lou et al., 2025; Neltner et al., 2014). 82 While these methods introduce precise geometric modeling (e.g., radial sampling), they are 83 optimized for non-Aβ pathologies and often operate on isolated image tiles. As a result, they are 84 ineffective at quantifying the co-localization of Aβ deposition within vessel walls, and they lack the 85 slide-level spatial context required to distinguish vascular compartments, key requirements for the 86 comprehensive assessment of CAA. 87 Furthermore, in prior works, the choice of DL model architecture has been a key limitation. Single 88 multi-class segmentation networks often suffer from inter-class competition in the presence of severe 89 class imbalance, leading to reduced sensitivity for small or sparse pathological targets such as 90 vascular Aβ deposits (Kervadec et al., 2021). Generalizability also remains a major concern. Many 91 pipelines are developed and evaluated using data from a single institution and are sensitive to 92 variability in staining, tissue processing, and scanning protocols, which restricts their applicability 93 across cohorts and centers (Khened et al., 2021). As a result, reproducible vessel-level quantification 94 and spatial characterization of CAA remain largely unavailable. 95

In this study, we present a DL pipeline for quantitative analysis of CAA in WSIs. We developed and 96 evaluated two custom segmentation architectures, one transformer-based and one convolutional 97 neural network (CNN)-based and compared their performance with a previously described standard 98 U-Net architecture (Ronneberger et al., 2015) across four targets: Aβ deposits, vessel walls, 99 leptomeningeal space (LS), and gray matter (GM). We trained independent binary models for each 100 target and fused their outputs into a composite semantic map. Using data from two institutions, we 101 assessed robustness across staining and scanning conditions. Finally, we utilized these maps to 102 spatially assign individual vessels to anatomical compartments and derive vessel-level morphometric 103 and Aβ burden measurements. This framework enables reproducible, vessel-level quantification of 104 CAA beyond conventional semiquantitative grading systems. 105 

2 Materials 106 

2.1 Cohort selection and tissue acquisition 107 

We obtained postmortem human brain tissue sections from 15 individuals with autopsy confirmed 108 AD through the Alzheimer’s Disease Research Centers (ADRC) at the University of Pittsburgh (Pitt) 109 (n = 10; internal training and validation data set) and the University of Kentucky (UKY) (n = 5; 110 independent external test data set). From each individual, we analyzed two cortical tissue sections, 111 one from the frontal cortex and one from the occipital cortex. Demographic characteristics and 112 neuropathologic assessments for the Pitt and UKY cohorts are summarized in Table 1 and Table 2, 113 respectively. Informed consent for autopsy and the research use of tissue was obtained from next-of-114 kin of all brain donors. The study was approved by the Committee for Oversight of Research and 115 Clinical Training Involving Decedents (CORID) at Pitt and the UKY Institutional Review Board. All 116 data were de-identified before analysis in accordance with institutional policies and applicable 117 privacy regulations. 118 Diagnosis of presence and severity of ADNC was based on established criteria (Montine et al., 2012), 119 incorporating Braak neurofibrillary tangle (NFT) staging (Braak et al., 2006), Thal Aβ phases (Thal 120 et al., 2002), Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) neuritic plaque 121 scores (Mirra et al., 1991), and overall ABC scores, as performed by a board-certified 122 neuropathologist. We sampled two cortical regions from the left cerebral hemisphere of each case: 123 the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex at the level of the midfrontal gyrus and the medial occipital cortex 124 including the primary visual cortex. This strategy yielded a total dataset of 30 tissue sections. Each 125 section comprised the full-thickness cortex, capturing cortical GM and underlying white matter 126 (WM). 127 

2.2 Tissue processing 128 

Sections from the Pitt cohort were cut at a thickness of 5 μm from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded 129 (FFPE) blocks. Slides were first deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated with methanol hydrogen 130 peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by pretreatment with 90% formic for 40 131 min. Aβ immunohistochemistry was performed using antibody NAB228 (1:4000; Cell Signaling 132 Technology, Danvers, MA) for 45 min at room temperature. Signal detection was carried out using a 133 biotinylated mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:200 for 30 minutes), the ABC Elite detection system, 134 and Nova Red chromogen. Slides were then counterstained with hematoxylin. No vessel-specific 135 immunohistochemical markers were applied. 136

3 Methods 153 

3.1 Annotation strategy 154 

All reference annotations were generated using QuPath version 5.1 (Bankhead et al., 2017). A single 155 annotator (H.T.) manually delineated regions of interest under the direct supervision of a board-156 certified neuropathologist (J.K.). The annotator produced binary pixel-level masks for four distinct 157 target classes: Aβ deposits, vessel walls, GM, and LS (Figure 1). To ensure comprehensive 158 segmentation, Aβ labels encompassed both vascular and parenchymal deposits, while vessel wall 159 labels included both unaffected and Aβ-laden vessels. These finalized masks served as the ground 160 truth for model training, validation, and testing. 161 

3.2 Tile extraction 162 

For Aβ and vessel wall segmentation tasks, we extracted image tiles at 40X magnification with tile 163 dimensions 512 x 512 pixels and no overlap, as these targets require high spatial resolution to capture 164 fine structural detail and sparse pathology. For GM and LS segmentation, we extracted tiles at 10X 165 magnification using the same pixel dimensions, as these tissue compartments span large contiguous 166 regions. We excluded tiles with minimal tissue content by applying a tissue coverage threshold of 5 167 percent. Tiles below this threshold were removed prior to model training, validation, and testing. 168

We implemented a five-fold cross-validation scheme on this 174 development set. Crucially, all tiles derived from a single WSI were assigned to the same partition 175

3.4 Data augmentation 187 

During training, we applied paired geometric transformations to input images and their 188 corresponding ground truth masks to preserve spatial correspondence. These transformations were 189 limited to random horizontal and vertical flips (Shorten & Khoshgoftaar, 2019). Following geometric 190 transformations and intensity scaling, we applied image-only appearance augmentation with a 191 probability of 0.5. This augmentation perturbed stain appearance in optical density space to simulate 192 staining and scanner-related variability and included Gaussian blur and additive noise (Tellez et al., 193 2019). We did not apply any data augmentation during validation or testing. Figure 3 shows 194 examples of data augmentation used during model training 195 

3.5 Model architectures 196 

We developed three U-Net-style semantic segmentation architectures. Each model mapped an RGB 197 (red, green, and blue) tile x ∈R3×H×W to a single channel logit map z ∈R1×H×W . A logit map is the raw 198 output of the network before conversion to probabilities. We converted these logits to pixel-wise 199 probabilities using a sigmoid function, y "=σ(z), which maps each pixel value to a range between 0 and 200 1 and allows the model to estimate the probability that each pixel belongs to the target class (Hao et 201 al., 2020). Table 5 details the computational specifications for each architecture, including the total 202 number of trainable parameters and floating-point operations (FLOPs), which estimate the amount of 203 computation required to process one input tile. 204 

3.5.1 Standard U-Net 205 

We implemented a standard U-Net architecture following the original design (Ronneberger et al., 206 2015). The network consisted of a symmetric encoder-decoder structure with skip connections 207 between corresponding resolution levels (Figure 4), in which the encoder extracts features, and the 208 decoder reconstructs the segmentation mask while the skip connections preserve fine spatial detail. 209 

3.5.1.1 Encoder 210 

The encoder included four stages with progressive spatial downsampling. Downsampling reduces the 211 spatial size of the feature maps while increasing the effective receptive field, which allows the model 212 to use a larger image context. Each stage applied two consecutive 3 x 3 convolutional layers followed 213 by batch normalization and Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation. Convolutional layers learn local 214 image patterns such as edges, textures, and shapes. Batch normalization stabilizes training by 215 standardizing feature values. ReLU is a nonlinear activation function that sets negative values to zero 216

3.5.1.2 Decoder 220 

The decoder mirrored the encoder and progressively restored spatial resolution. At each stage, feature 221 maps were upsampled and concatenated with the corresponding encoder features. The fused features 222 were refined using two consecutive convolutional layers with batch normalization and ReLU 223 activation. A final 1 x 1 convolution produced a single-channel logit map for segmentation. 224 

3.5.2 Dual-attention residual U-Net 225 

The first model extended the standard U-Net architecture (Ronneberger et al., 2015) by incorporating 226 residual learning (He et al., 2016) and attention mechanisms to enhance feature representation while 227 preserving spatial detail (Woo et al., 2018). Residual learning uses shortcut connections to help 228 information and gradients pass more easily through the network during training. Figure 5 shows an 229 overview of this model architecture. 230 

3.5.2.1 Encoder 231 

The encoder consisted of four stages with progressive spatial downsampling. We designed a hybrid 232 block strategy to optimize gradient flow at different scales. In the shallow encoder stages (stages 1 233 and 2), we applied three consecutive convolutional blocks, each composed of a 3 x 3 convolution, 234 batch normalization, and Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) activation (Purwanti et al., 2025). We 235 used GELU because it is a smoother activation function than ReLU and often supports stable 236 optimization in modern architectures. In the deeper encoder stages (stages 3 and 4), we replaced 237 standard convolutional blocks with post-activation residual blocks to facilitate gradient propagation 238 and stabilize training at higher semantic levels (He et al., 2016) (Figure 6). Inspired by convolutional 239 block attention module (CBAM)-Residual U-Net, we applied a CBAM after each encoder stage and 240 before downsampling (Shah & Kang, 2023). CBAM is a lightweight attention module that 241 sequentially applies channel attention and spatial attention. Channel attention helps the model weigh 242 which feature channels are most informative, whereas spatial attention helps the model emphasize 243 where the important signal is located within the image. The module applied channel attention 244 followed by spatial attention (Woo et al., 2018). 245 

3.5.2.2 Decoder 246 

The decoder progressively recovered spatial dimensions using an upsampling scheme designed to 247 mitigate the checkerboard artifacts often associated with transposed convolutions (Kinoshita & Kiya, 248 2020). At each decoding stage, we applied bilinear interpolation with a scale factor of two, followed 249 by a 1 x 1 convolution to reduce channel dimensionality (Zhang et al., 2019). We concatenated the 250 upsampled features with the corresponding encoder features through skip connections. The fused 251 features were refined using three consecutive 3 x 3 convolutions, batch normalization, and GELU 252 layers. A final 1 x 1 convolution mapped the decoder features to a single-channel logit map. 253 

3.5.3 Hybrid Swin transformer U-Net 254 

The second model replaced the convolutional encoder with a hierarchical transformer encoder based 255 on Swin Transformer V2 (Liu et al., 2022). Inspired by SwinUNETR-V2, convolutional layers were 256 integrated into the architecture to reinforce local spatial coherence and stabilize training (He et al., 257 2023). 3.5.3.1 Patch embedding 259 

The model partitioned each input image into nonoverlapping 3 x 3 patches. This step breaks the 260 image into small local regions so they can be processed as tokens by the transformer. A strided 261 convolution projected these patches into a higher-dimensional embedding space, preserving spatial 262 structure while converting local neighborhoods into token representations. 263 

3.5.3.2 Encoder 264 

Each encoder stage began with a pre-activation residual convolutional block (Figure 8). This block 265 applied instance normalization and GELU activation prior to a 3 x 3 convolution. Instance 266 normalization normalizes each image sample independently, which can be helpful when feature 267 statistics vary across samples. This design stabilized feature statistics and reinforced local spatial 268 structure before attention-based processing (Wu et al., 2021). Following convolutional refinement, 269 features were processed using Swin Transformer V2 blocks with window-based multi-head self-270 attention. In this setting, self-attention allows the model to compare information across different 271 image locations, while the window-based design limits this operation to local regions to reduce 272 computational cost. Attention alternated between non-shifted and cyclically shifted windows to 273 enable information exchange across window boundaries while maintaining linear computational 274 complexity with respect to image size (Liu et al., 2022). 275 Each transformer block followed a residual formulation and included layer normalization and a 276 multilayer perceptron with GELU activation. Stochastic depth was applied to residual paths during 277 training to regularize deep representations. Stochastic depth randomly drops some residual paths 278 during training and acts as a form of regularization. Between encoder stages, the model applied patch 279 merging. This operation concatenated neighboring tokens in a space-to-depth manner and projected 280 them through a linear layer, reducing spatial resolution while increasing channel capacity. 281 

3.5.3.3 Decoder 282 

The decoder mirrored the encoder hierarchy. At each level, bilinear upsampling doubled the spatial 283 resolution, followed by convolution to reduce channel dimensionality. Skip features from the encoder 284 were first processed using residual convolutional blocks to align semantic representations before 285 concatenation. This step helped make the encoder and decoder features more compatible before 286 fusion. The fused features were then refined using additional residual convolutional blocks. A final 1 287 x 1 convolution produced the binary segmentation logit map. 288 We computed FLOPs using a standard input tile size of 512 x 512 pixels. Table 5 lists the 289 computational cost and the total number of trainable parameters for the U-Net, Swin-UNet, and DA-290 ResUNet architectures. 291

3.8 Multi-resolution inference strategy 326

We utilized the four trained binary segmentation models targeting vessel walls, Aβ deposits, GM, 327 and LS to process WSIs. To balance segmentation accuracy with computational efficiency, we 328 performed inference at different spatial resolutions of the image pyramid depending on the target 329 structure. GM and LS were segmented at a downsampled pyramid level corresponding to a fourfold 330 reduction in resolution, as these compartments span large contiguous regions and do not require 331 cellular-level detail. In contrast, vessel wall and Aβ segmentation were performed at native image 332 resolution to preserve fine structural detail and to capture sparse Aβ deposits and vessel 333 morphometry. We performed inference using a sliding window approach with tiles of size 2048 x 334 2048 pixels and an overlap of 1024 pixels. The overlap reduced edge effects at tile boundaries and 335 improved spatial continuity in the reconstructed predictions. To increase robustness at inference time, 336 we applied test-time augmentation (TTA) (Moshkov et al., 2020). For each tile, we generated 337 predictions from the original image and from its horizontally and vertically flipped versions. We 338 averaged these predictions to produce the final probability map for each tile. 339 

3.9 Composite mask construction 340 

We synthesized a single composite segmentation map by fusing the outputs from the four 341 independent binary models using a bitwise encoding strategy. We assigned a unique power-of-two 342 label to each model prediction: LS (2 0=1), GM (2 1=2), vessel wall (2 2=4), and Aβ (2 3=8). Prior to 343 fusion, we upsampled the predictions generated at lower resolutions (GM and LS) to match the native 344 resolution of the vessel and Aβ masks. We combined all predictions using a logical OR operation, 345 producing an overlap map that preserved concurrent classifications at the pixel level. For example, 346 pixels classified as both vessel wall and Aβ deposit were assigned a value of 12. Pixels containing 347 both vessel wall and Aβ labels were subsequently classified as Aβ immunohistochemistry (IHC)-348 positive vessels. Regions not segmented by either the LS or GM models were implicitly assigned to 349 WM. This rule-based post-processing step enabled explicit separation of vascular Aβ deposits from 350 parenchymal Aβ plaques and from Aβ IHC-negative vessels. The sequential application of models 351 and fusion of their outputs into a composite, color-coded segmentation mask is illustrated in Figure 9. 352 

3.10 Vessel instance extraction from composite masks 353 

We derived all vascular morphometrics directly from the composite, color-coded segmentation 354 masks, which encoded vessel walls, Aβ deposits, LS, GM, and WM. All downstream measurements 355 were computed deterministically from these masks and did not require access to the original 356 histology images. We performed connected-component labeling on the vessel wall class and 357 extracted contour hierarchies using OpenCV 4.12 to recover nested anatomical structures. We 358 defined a valid vessel instance as an inner lumen contour fully enclosed by a corresponding outer 359 vessel wall contour. We excluded candidates that failed predefined geometric validity criteria, such 360 as missing enclosure or implausible contour geometry. 361

3.11.2 Tangent-normal sampling-based measurements 375 

In this approach, we measured vessel wall thickness by sampling points along the boundary of the 376 lumen. At each lumen boundary point P i, we computed the local tangent vector t and computed the 377 outward normal vector as the perpendicular direction to the tangent (Figure 10). We then calculated 378 the normal wall thickness (T tangent ) by measuring the Euclidean distance along this normal vector 379 from the inner lumen boundary to its intersection with the outer vessel wall. This approach captures 380 local thickness variations independent of the vessel center, providing robust measurements for 381 elongated or deformed lumens. 382

For each vessel, we summarized the variability of 385 radial distances from the lumen centroid to the outer vessel wall across angular directions. 3.12 Quantification of vascular Aβ burden and spatial distribution 392 

We quantified vascular Aβ burden as the proportion of Aβ-labeled pixels within the segmented vessel 393 wall. To characterize the peri-vascular spatial distribution of the deposits, we performed a concentric 394 annular analysis. We generated a single ring-shaped region of interest (ROI) by morphologically 395 dilating the outer vessel wall boundary to a fixed radial distance of 75 μm. This distance was adopted 396 from a prior neuropathological study that defined the perivascular compartment as a fixed-radius 397 region extending from the tunica externa for quantification of Aβ signal (Fastenau et al., 2025). 398 Within this annulus, we computed the Aβ density, defined as the ratio of Aβ-labeled pixels to the 399 total pixel count of the region. 400 To evaluate whether vascular Aβ deposition was circumferential or focal, we partitioned the vessel 401 wall into angular sectors aligned with the radial sampling framework. We classified vessels as 402 circumferentially affected when Aβ signal exceeded a 50% occupancy threshold across all sectors. 403 This classification distinguished uniformly encircling deposition from segmental or patchy 404 involvement. 405

4.1 Segmentation performance and model comparison 413 

4.1.1 Segmentation performance on the internal development cohort 414 

We evaluated segmentation performance for vessel walls, Aβ deposits, GM, and LS using five-fold 415 cross-validation on the internal development cohort. We compared standard U-Net, DA-ResUNet, 416 and Swin U-Net using Dice coefficient, recall, and precision. Mean values and standard deviations 417 (SD) across folds are reported in Table 7. 418 All three architectures achieved high segmentation fidelity, with Dice scores exceeding 90% for all 419 tissue compartments. The Swin-UNet demonstrated the highest performance for vascular 420 segmentation, achieving a Dice score of 91.6±1.3%, compared to 91.4±1.4% for the DA-ResUNet 421 and 90.6±1.7% for the Standard U-Net. 422 For fine-grained pathological features, the DA-ResUNet exhibited a slight advantage. In the amyloid 423 segmentation task, the DA-ResUNet achieved the highest Dice score ( 95.0±1.7% ) and Precision 424 (95.5±1.2%). Swin-UNet showed comparable but slightly lower performance with a Dice score of 425 94.7±1.7% and Precision of 95.2±1.2%. 426 Notably, the Standard U-Net remained highly competitive, particularly for large-scale structural 427 regions. For LS, it achieved the highest Dice score of (99.3±1.0%), outperforming both the Swin-428 UNet (98.8±2.2%) and the DA-ResUNet (97.6±4.9%). This indicates that for distinct, macroscopic 429 tissue boundaries, the added complexity of attention mechanisms may not be necessary for optimal 430 segmentation. For GM, both advanced architectures performed identically (97 .8 ± 1.0% ) slightly 431 surpassing the Standard U-Net (97.4±0.9%). 432 Visual inspection of the segmentation masks confirmed that both the DA-ResUNet and Swin-UNet 433 produced sharper boundaries than the Standard U-Net (Figure 11). We also note that the reference 434 annotations are not error free, and in some cases the model predictions corrected minor 435 inconsistencies or imprecision in the ground truth delineations. 436 Based on this performance and its computational efficiency, we selected DA-ResUNet as the 437 segmentation backbone for all subsequent vessel-level and morphometric analyses. 438

4.2 Detection and quality filtering of individual blood vessels 454 

The pipeline successfully detected individual blood vessels across GM, WM, and LS based on the 455 presence of a continuous vessel wall enclosing a clearly defined lumen. Detected vessels exhibited 456 anatomically plausible geometry and were suitable for downstream morphometric analysis. 457 Structures that did not meet these criteria were automatically excluded, most commonly due to 458 fragmented or discontinuous vessel walls that prevented reliable identification of an enclosed lumen. 459 Furthermore, we applied a minimum lumen area threshold of 20μm 2 to exclude candidate vessels 460 with poorly resolved lumens at the available image resolution, while retaining valid capillary-scale 461 vessels. This defined minimum threshold is modifiable by users. Representative examples of 462 successfully detected vessels and excluded non-vascular artifacts are shown in Figure 12. 463 

4.3 Validation of geometric characterization and CAA identification 464 

Vessel-level identification of CAA was performed by spatial co-localization of vessel wall and 465 amyloid segmentation masks. For each detected vessel, the pipeline computed inner and outer 466 diameters, the inner-to-outer diameter ratio, vessel roundness, and wall thickness using both radial 467 (T radial ) and tangent-normal (T tangent ) measurements. Aβ burden was quantified within the vessel wall 468 and within a perivascular ring-shaped annulus to capture Aβ extending beyond the vessel boundary. 469 Based on the angular distribution of Aβ signal, vessels were further classified as circumferentially 470 affected or focally involved. Representative examples of these vessel-level measurements are shown 471 in Figure 13. As a preliminary validation of the pipeline, occipital cortex sections showed higher 472 vessel amyloid burden, more circumferential vessel involvement, and more Aβ-positive capillary 473 vessels than frontal cortex sections. 474 

5 Discussion 475 

In this study, we developed and evaluated a DL-based framework for automated, vessel-level 476 analysis of cerebrovascular pathology in WSIs of human post-mortem brain tissue. The pipeline 477 combines DL-based segmentation with deterministic geometric feature extraction to enable 478 quantitative assessment of blood vessel morphology and vascular Aβ involvement. We compared 479 convolutional and transformer-based segmentation architectures, assessed generalization across 480 independent external cohorts, and demonstrated reliable detection of individual vessels across GM, 481 WM, and LS. By deriving all measurements directly from composite segmentation masks, the 482 approach supports scalable and reproducible vessel-level quantification without reliance on semi-483 quantitative grading systems. This framework provides a generalizable foundation for computational 484 analysis of CAA and related vascular pathologies in WSIs. 485

We evaluated three segmentation architectures, standard U-Net, DA-ResUNet, and Swin-UNet, to 486 examine trade-offs between segmentation accuracy, generalization, and computational efficiency in 487 WSI analysis. The Swin-UNet showed nominally higher performance for vascular segmentation, 488 which is consistent with the ability of transformer-based self-attention to model long-range spatial 489 dependencies and preserve continuity in elongated structures beyond the local receptive fields of 490 conventional convolutional networks (Thisanke et al., 2023). Such global context is advantageous for 491 maintaining vessel integrity across large fields of view. However, this representational capacity 492 comes at a substantial computational cost. Self-attention mechanisms remain more resource-intensive 493 than convolutional operations, even when constrained through window-based and shifted-window 494 attention as implemented in Swin Transformers (Liu et al., 2021; Palanisamy et al., 2025). In our 495 implementation, this resulted in a markedly higher computational overhead: while the DA-ResUNet 496 processed a typical WSI (approx. 80,000 x 80,000 pixels) in approximately 4-5 minutes, the Swin-497 UNet required nearly 8-10 minutes under identical hardware conditions. This 2-fold increase in 498 processing time implies that despite comparable theoretical FLOPs, the memory access patterns of 499 the Transformer architecture create a significant bottleneck, potentially limiting its viability for high-500 throughput clinical workflows where rapid case turnover is essential. 501 In contrast, the DA-ResUNet achieved a more favorable balance between performance and 502 efficiency. Its residual design improves gradient flow and feature reuse, while the integration of 503 channel and spatial attention selectively emphasizes informative regions and suppresses background 504 noise. This architectural bias is well suited for detecting sparse, fine-grained pathological features 505 such as vascular Aβ deposits, where subtle intensity and texture cues are critical. Similar 506 observations have been reported in other medical imaging domains, where attention-augmented 507 residual U-Net variants improved lesion segmentation by expanding the effective receptive field and 508 enhancing multi-scale feature integration without incurring the computational burden of transformer 509 backbones (Sen et al., 2025; Shah & Kang, 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). For example, attention-510 enhanced residual U-Net models have demonstrated improved performance over standard U-Net 511 architectures in breast ultrasound lesion segmentation, particularly for small or poorly defined 512 targets, while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for clinical workflows (Zhang, 2025). 513 Evaluation on an independent external cohort revealed differences in model robustness under domain 514 shift that reflect architectural inductive biases. The Standard U-Net showed reduced stability in 515 heterogeneous tissue regions, likely due to reliance on low-level appearance features such as staining 516 intensity and local texture, which vary across scanners and sites. In contrast, the DA-ResUNet and 517 Swin-UNet maintained more consistent behavior, suggesting that attention mechanisms promote 518 greater reliance on structural and spatial features rather than pixel-level appearance. Inter-site 519 variability is a critical issue in multi-center histopathology because differences in tissue processing, 520 staining, and digitization can introduce large appearance shifts that are unrelated to biology and often 521 degrade model generalization (Asadi-Aghbolaghi et al., 2024; Tellez et al., 2019). 522 Unsurprisingly, the models showed different training behaviors. The Swin-UNet converged faster 523 during early training but began to overfit at later epochs, as validation loss increased while training 524 loss continued to decrease. This behavior is consistent with previous studies showing that 525 transformer-based models, due to their high model capacity, can overfit when training data are 526 limited (Touvron et al., 2021). In contrast, the DA-ResUNet showed more stable training over longer 527 schedules, while the standard U-Net required more epochs to converge. 528 We next evaluated the sequential use of multiple binary segmentation models and the generation of a 529 composite, color-coded segmentation mask. This design avoided the class imbalance challenges that 530

Training 531 one model per target minimized class competition and allowed explicit control of model-specific 532 errors. We adjusted the loss function asymmetrically, penalizing FPs more strongly for the Aβ model 533 to limit spurious CAA detection, and penalizing FNs more strongly for the vessel wall model to favor 534 complete vessel identification. The primary limitation of this approach is increased inference time 535 compared with a single multi-class model. 536 The pipeline’s vessel detection prioritizes geometric validity over maximal sensitivity. By requiring a 537 continuous vessel wall and a detectable lumen, the framework effectively excluded nonvascular 538 artifacts and isolated false-positive pixels, which is critical for reliable downstream morphometric 539 analysis. Although vessels with fragmented walls or small lumens may be missed, this trade-off is 540 acceptable in a vessel-centric analysis where false positives can propagate substantial bias into 541 geometric measurements. The deterministic quality filtering, together with asymmetric loss 542 weighting during training, enabled consistent vessel identification while maintaining biological 543 plausibility. 544 Vessel morphometry presents additional challenges due to sectioning artifacts and shape variability. 545 Tangentially cut vessels or vessels with highly asymmetric geometry can distort diameter and 546 thickness estimates when measured using purely radial assumptions. In the analyses of these 547 structures, large discrepancies between opposing radial measurements serve as indicators of 548 geometric asymmetry. The pipeline addresses this variability through rule-based handling, including 549 conservative diameter estimation and the use of tangent-normal thickness measurements when radial 550 estimates become unreliable. Measuring wall thickness perpendicular to the lumen boundary reduces 551 sensitivity to lumen eccentricity and better captures focal wall remodeling. These complementary 552 strategies allow robust geometric quantification across diverse vessel shapes while preserving 553 interpretability and minimizing bias introduced by histological sectioning artifacts. 554 Quantification of vascular Aβ burden captured both intramural deposition and perivascular extension. 555 Estimating Aβ fraction within the vessel wall provided a direct measure of vascular involvement, 556 while analysis of a ring-shaped perivascular region enabled detection of Aβ extending into the 557 adjacent neuropil. This pattern is characteristic of dyshoric CAA, where Aβ deposition spreads 558 beyond the vessel wall into surrounding tissue (Richard et al., 2010). Incorporating this spatial 559 context allows the pipeline to distinguish Aβ immunoreactivity that is confined to the vessel wall 560 from parenchymal Aβ deposition that extends into the surrounding tissue. Unlike conventional 561 semiquantitative grading, which is based on visual assessment of a limited number of vessels, this 562 framework provides exact vessel-level measurements of vascular Aβ burden, perivascular Aβ burden, 563 and vessel geometry across all analyzable vessels in a section. For this reason, the pipeline is best 564 viewed as a quantitative complement to routine neuropathologic assessment. In particular, it can 565 serve as a research tool for large-scale, reproducible extraction of vascular features that are not 566 practical to obtain manually. These measurements can support identification of vessel-level patterns, 567 clustering of CAA phenotypes, and comparisons across brain regions, cohorts, and disease states. For 568 example, in the present cohort, the pipeline identified more circumferentially affected vessels, more 569 Aβ-positive capillary vessels, and higher average vascular amyloid burden in the occipital cortex than 570 in the frontal cortex. These observations are consistent with prior biologic studies showing that CAA 571 is more severe in the occipital cortex (Attems et al., 2005). 572 The current study has several limitations. Vessel detection relied on strict geometric rules that favor 573 specificity over sensitivity. At vessel bifurcation sites, where two lumens may be present, or in 574 regions where adjacent vessel walls are in close contact, the pipeline retained only the vessel with the 575

While this strategy effectively removed nonvascular artifacts, it may result in 577 missed detections in complex vascular regions. In addition, although the models demonstrated good 578 generalizability across the institutions we tested, larger variations in staining or scanner 579 characteristics could hinder performance. Of note, our models were developed for Aβ immunostained 580 sections only. Other staining protocols, such as hematoxylin and eosin, require separate evaluation 581 and potential fine-tuning. We also did not perform formal cross-magnification validation. Vessel wall 582 and Aβ models were trained and validated at 40X, whereas GM and LS models were trained and 583 validated at 10X. Lower effective resolution may reduce sensitivity for very small vessels, 584 particularly capillaries. The sample size was also limited, and broader patient-level and institution-585 level cohort diversity may reveal additional variability in vessel morphology and Aβ deposition 586 patterns. Quantification of perivascular Aβ was based on a fixed ring-shaped annulus at a predefined 587 distance from the vessel wall, which may not capture parenchymal Aβ in cases with enlarged 588 perivascular spaces. Finally, given that a single Aβ IHC-positive pixel within a vessel wall is 589 sufficient to classify a vessel as CAA-positive in our implementation, this framework favors 590 sensitivity and may overestimate vascular involvement in borderline cases. 591 Future work will address these limitations by expanding training data to include larger cohorts and 592 greater staining variability, which may improve robustness and refine vessel- and Aβ-level 593 characterization. We also plan to provide model checkpoints trained at different resolutions so that 594 users can select the most appropriate model for their scanning workflow. Additional rules or 595 probabilistic criteria could be incorporated to better handle bifurcations, fragmented walls, and 596 ambiguous vessel geometries. The modular design of the pipeline also allows integration of 597 additional models to detect related pathologies, such as microhemorrhages, infarctions, and immune 598 cell infiltration, enabling more comprehensive characterization of CAA-associated tissue changes. 599 Ultimately, the resulting quantitative vessel-level features could support definition of CAA 600 endophenotypes and facilitate analysis of their relationships with clinical presentation, disease 601 progression, and genetic factors. Beyond CAA, the same strategy may also support quantitative study 602 of other small-vessel diseases, including arteriolosclerosis. 603 In conclusion, we present a modular DL framework for vessel-level analysis of CAA in WSIs. By 604 combining target-specific segmentation with deterministic geometric measurements, the pipeline 605 enables reproducible quantification of vascular structure and Aβ immunoreactivity involvement at 606 scale. The design emphasizes interpretability, robustness, and flexibility, providing a foundation for 607 future extensions to additional vascular pathologies and integrative analyses linking imaging-derived 608 features with clinical and genetic data. 609

Patch embedding and successive encoder stages capture multi-scale contextual features, 858 while skip connections and fusion convolutions integrate encoder and decoder representations. The 859 model produces a single-channel segmentation mask through a final 1 x 1 convolutional output head. 860
