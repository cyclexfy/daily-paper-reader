# Abstract 16 

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) has revolutionized our understanding of tumor biology but 17 

inherently lacks information on the upstream somatic driver mutations. We developed  a18 

spatially -aware  graph  convolutional neural network  (MuT -GCNN)  that infers  TP53  clones 19 

directly from ST  data.  MuT -GCNN was trained  on  virtual ST slides  with clones  simulated from 20 

a large collection of  existing  RNA and  matched  DNA sequencing data.  The model  is  highly 21 

performant with precision and recall values exceeding 9 5% in  most  analysed  cancer types.  It 22 

is sensitive  for  single hit mutations and is primarily informed by the expression of p53 signalling 23 

genes in cancer cells.  After  demonstrat ing  th e potential  of the model  on  publicly  available 24 

squamous cell carcinoma  (SCC)  data , a direct  validat ion was performed  using ST and 25 

matched  DNA sequencing  from  serial slices obtained from 4  cutaneous  SCC samples. With 26 

the  increasing availability of ST data and upcoming ST atlases, MuT -GCNN  can  unveil  the 27 

location of  (sub) clonal alterations in  TP53 , the most frequently mutated gene in human cancer. 28

# Introduction 29 

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) has successfully integrated the microscopic and 30 

transcriptomic information layers, providing a more holistic understanding of tissue properties. 31 

Systematic analyses of whole -transcriptome ST data derived from sequence -based 32 

technologies (e.g.,  Visium  (HD ),  Slide -Seq (V2)  or  Stereo -Seq ) provided new insights in the 33 

tumor  and its  microenvironment including paracrine signa lling , tumor -immune interactions and 34 

cellular subpopulations and niches  (1 –3) .35 

While an increasing amount of these tumor ST data are being made publicly available, 36 

resulting in secondary discoveries  (4 –6) , they inherently lack key information on the upstream 37 

genomic events that are driving the tumor. Copy number alterations (CNAs) can still be 38 

inferred from ST data by combining genomic information with gene expression changes  (7,8) .39 

However, this is not possible for single nucleotide variants (SNVs). 40 

It was previously suggested that  mutations  in canonical cancer driver genes such as 41 

TP53  and  Ras  pathway  oncogenes  (KRAS, HRAS  and  NRAS ) can be predicted using 42 

machine learning models applied on bulk transcriptomics data  (9,10) . However, to our 43 

knowledge, such prediction models have not been created on ST data. Given the (sub)clonal 44 

nature of cells carrying driver mutations, neighboring spots on an ST slide have a higher 45 

probability to carry the same mutation and we hypothesize that the incorporation of this spatial 46 

information vastly increases the prediction accuracy. 47 

We focused on the p53  protein  encod ed by the  TP53  gene . TP53  is located on 48 

chromosome 17p13.1 and  is mutated in about half of all tumors and one of the best -studied 49 

tumor suppressor genes  (11 –13) . It’s a  transcription factor central in the regulation of  cellular 50 

response to DNA damage and stress, playing a crucial role in regulating cell cycle arrest, DNA 51 

damage repair and apoptosis  (14 –16) . The activity of  p53  is tightly regulated, primarily through 52 

its negative regulator  Mdm 2, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that targets p53 for degradation and 53 

controls its stability under normal conditions  (17 –19) . Functional p53 is crucial for genomic 54

the genome  (22) . TP53  mutations are often correlated with poor prognosis and aggressive 56 

tumor characteristics  (23 –25) . Somatic mutations in related  genes have been shown to result 57 

in downstream phenotypic consequences that resemble those caused by mutated  TP53 . Such 58 

TP53 -like alterations are referred to as phenocopies and include alterations in  MDM2 , MDM4 ,59 

PPM1D  or  USP28  (10,26 –31) .60 

To predict  TP53  mutations in ST data, we developed MuT -GCNN (Mutant TP53 -Graph 61 

Convolutional Neural Network), a graph convolutional neural network (GCNN) that inherently 62 

models the spatial relation between neighboring spots. We demonstrate that the model 63 

accurately predicts simulated  TP53  subclones and  has  superior  performance  compared to 64 

non -spatial  approaches . Furthermore, we show that MuT -GCNN generalizes across cancer 65 

types and independent datasets and captures biologically meaningful transcriptional programs 66 

associated with  TP53  dysfunction.  Finally , we validated the predicted TP53 mutations using 67 

ST and  matched  DNA sequencing on sequential sections obtained from 4 cutaneous 68 

squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) samples.  Together, these results highlight the potential of 69 

MuT -GCNN as a  methodological  framework for spatially resolving somatic  TP53  mutations 70 

from transcriptomic data in the absence of direct spatial genomic  profiles .71

# Results 72 

MuT -GCNN is trained on simulated spatial transcriptomics slides 73 

Training a deep learning model to predict somatic mutations from ST data would require the 74 

availability of matched ST and spatially resolved genomics reference data. As spatial 75 

genomics technologies are still in their infancy  (7,32) , such training data are not (yet) available. 76 

To overcome this problem, we opted to train the model on  virtual  ST slides,  generated from 77 

matched bulk DNA and RNA sequencing data . Bulk RNA -seq data were downloaded from 78 

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) along with the mutation status of the  TP53  gene (n = 6563; 79 

samples with both expression and mutation data).  Subjects  were randomly grouped in  a80 

training (70%) and test  cohort  (30%). After simulating 7  TP53  subclones on an  in -silico 81 

generated virtual  ST slide,  cancer type -specific  TCGA training data were randomly assigned 82 

to ST spots  while  considering  TP53  mutation status . Finally, the grid of spots was converted 83 

to an undirected graph by connecting each spot to its  neighbouring  spots to form edges 84 

(connections) and nodes (spots) . These graphs were used to train  a graph convolutional 85 

neural network (GCNN) followed by a multilayer perceptron (MLP)  for binary classification of 86 

each node  (Fig. 1) .87

spots. 99

MuT -GCNN accurately predicts single and double hit  TP53  mutations in different 100 

cancer types 101 

The prediction accuracy of the trained model was determined using the test data (n =  1979). 102 

For each  individual  cancer type  as well as the mixed, pan -cancer dataset,  100  ST  test  slides 103 

were simulated and fed to the model for  binary  prediction  of each spot  (TP53  mutated or wild -104 

type). The model accurately predicted  TP53  mutation status for the pan -cancer datasets 105 

(precision  0.9 65 ; recall  0.9 71; Fig.  2a-b) as well as the 12 individually  analysed  TCGA cancer 106 

types (median precision  0. 96 8; median recall  0. 96 1). Recall was lowest for SKCM  (0.85 6)107 

while STAD  had the lowest precision value  (0.89 4; Fig. 2b) .108 

If these predictions are indeed  TP53  mutation specific, one would expect better prediction 109 

accuracies for more damaging mutations (e.g.,  frameshift  or nonsense  mutations). T o test this 110 

hypothesis, we simulated 100 ST slides with  a single clone containing  only  samples with  one 111 

specific  type of  TP53  mutation. Given the relatively low number of samples in each  mutation 112 

category , we  only  performed this analysis on the pan -cancer level. Higher  recall values  were 113 

found for  truncating mutations  (median  0.91 9) than for missense mutations (median  0.89 2; P114 

= 7.19e -3; one -sided  Mann -Whitney  U test ), confirming our hypothesis. As expected,  recall 115 

values  were near zero for silent mutations (median  0; Fig.  2c). 116 

TP53  is generally known as a haploinsufficient tumor suppressor gene  (11) , implying a dosage 117 

effect with larger expected downstream expression alterations when both alleles are affected 118 

(double hits) as compared to single allele alterations (single hits). To determine the sensitivity 119 

of the MuT -GCNN model to different allele number alterations, we grouped  TP53  mutated 120 

samples in single and dual hits based on their  TP53  copy number state, with  TP53  mutations 121 

in copy number neutral samples considered as single hits and in hemizygously deleted 122 

samples as double hits. Using the 100 ST slide pan -cancer approach, MuT -GCNN accurately 123 

detected samples with a single hit (median  recall  0.81 1). Interestingly, the  prediction score 124 

was significantly higher in samples with a double hit (median  recall  0.94 6, P = 6.75 e-22 , one -125 

sided  Mann -Whitney  U test). In contrast, samples that were hemizygously  TP53  deleted in the 126

# Discussion 293 

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) is revolutionizing our understanding of tumor biology but 294 

inherently lacks information on the upstream somatic driver mutations. We developed a 295 

spatially -aware graph convolutional neural network (MuT -GCNN) that  identifies (sub)clonal 296 

mutations in  TP53 , the most frequently mutated gene in human cancer  (11,13) .297

In the absence of large collections of spatial genomics  reference  datasets , we  trained MuT -298 

GCNN  on  in silico  generated,  virtual ST slide s with simulated  TP53  clones  using bulk tumor 299 

sequencing data from TCGA . Virtual slide s were designed following the default  Visium  layout 300 

(65 x 65 spots) and were  generated  by  randomly assigning  (bulk)  cancer type -specific  gene 301 

expression data from  TP53  mutated and wild -type samples to  corresponding  spot s. This 302 

methodology implies placing  heterogeneous  expression  data  from different patients and 303 

tumors in close spatial proximity. While this approach affect s the realism of the spatial context 304 

in the training data,  the incorporation of the spatial  context  during MuT -GCNN training implied 305 

that each spot considered the expression information of 24 neighbouring spots (two 306 

convolutional layers), which  is expected to remove any variation that was not related to  TP53 307 

mutation status, the  only  common spatial context denominator.  This likely explains the good 308 

generalizability across different cancer types and independent datasets. 309 

The added value of the spatial context  to  the model’s performance  was  further demonstrated 310 

by the superior prediction accuracy of MuT -GCNN as compared to two non -spatial models 311 

(i.e., unconnected GCNN and GLM) . Surprisingly, the unconnected GCNN model performed 312 

worse than  the  GLM, showing that the GCNN architecture alone does not provide any intrinsic 313 

advantage.  These results suggest that , while classical linear models are a better alternative 314 

for mutation predictions in the absence of any spatial context,  GCNN s have the potential to 315 

improve performance without relying on post -processing  spatial  clustering steps , in line with 316 

their popularity  for deep learning on spatial transcriptomics  (62) .317 

A key challenge when developing any novel machine learning approach is the “black box” 318 

phenomenon, i.e. does MuT -GCNN indeed predict the expected  TP53  mutations or is the 319 

performance explained and/or biased by hidden, correlated variables? To address this 320 

challenge, w e demonstrated the  TP53  specificity using  three  orthogonal approaches : at the 321 

genomic, cellular and the molecular layer.  Firstly, when evaluating the model on simulated 322 

slides  that were homogenous for prespecified  mutation types, the best recall values were 323 

observed for high impact mutations, which are expected to have the largest impact on the 324

Design and training  of the graph convolutional network 452 

A graph convolutional neural network (GCNN) is a neural network designed to operate on 453 

graph -structured data. It can aggregate and transform information from a node’s features, its 454 

neighbours and the connections between them to learn representations. MuT -GCNN is tuned 455 

to perform node prediction. The GCNN is made up of  two  subsequent graph convolution 456 

layers, implemented in  Pytorch Geometric  as  GCNConv  (77) . The first  GCNConv  layer 457 

receives a matrix of gene expression values for each spatial location or spot, as well as the 458 

expression value matrix  associated with the surrounding spots. These surrounding locations 459 

are defined  in  the adjacency matrix  (A) . The values  of A were  set to zero  for the unconnected 460 

GCNN. The second  GCNConv  layer performs another convolution sequentially. This output is 461 

passed to a multilayer perceptron (MLP) consisting of  five  subsequent layers, steadily 462 

reducing the data dimensionality to a single prediction value which undergoes a sigmoid 463 

transformation. The output of each layer in the model is transformed by a  rectified linear unit 464 

(ReLu ) activation function. The model contains  three  dropout layers with a dropout rate of 465 

50%, 20% and 50% respectively placed between the  GCNConv  layers and the first  three  MLP 466 

layers. 467 

The model was trained for 70 epochs while monitoring the loss of the training dataset. 468 

Historically, an epoch is defined as one entire passing of the training data through the 469 

algorithm.  In this context,  the  classical definition of an  epoch  should be  adapted , as  most 470 

samples are reused multiple times to create one simulated ST slide.  Thus, w e define one 471 

epoch  as one entire passing of all simulated ST slides through the GCNN. The order of slides 472
