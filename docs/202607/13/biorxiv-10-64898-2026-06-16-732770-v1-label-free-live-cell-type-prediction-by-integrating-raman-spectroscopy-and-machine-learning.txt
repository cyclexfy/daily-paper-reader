Abstract 25 

Coherent Raman spectroscopy enables label-free biochemical fingerprinting of live 26 

cells with subcellular resolution. We previously developed a machine learning 27 

framework capable of classifying glioma FFPE tissues using Raman spectral 28 

signatures. To accelerate live cell acquisition, we previously developed RADAR 29 

(Raman Spectral Analysis Using Deep Learning for Artifact Removal), a method that 30 

increases imaging speed by an order of magnitude while preserving spectral integrity. 31 

By integrating high-speed Raman imaging with supervised machine learning, we 32 

aimed to define unique biochemical fingerprints specific to cell type. We hypothesized 33 

that intrinsic biochemical composition alone is sufficient to distinguish cellular identity 34 

and tumor subtype. To test this, we generated metabolic maps of diverse brain-derived 35 

cell types—including astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, and glioblastoma cells—using 36 

coherent Raman spectroscopy at single-cell resolution. Patient-derived brain tumor 37 

cell lines representing genetically heterogeneous backgrounds were analyzed. 38 

Samples were stratified by IDH1 mutation status (IDH1-mutant and IDH1–wild-type) 39 

and histologically classified as oligodendroglioma or astrocytoma. Raman spectral 40 

data were acquired from 286 live single cells across the two principal molecular 41 

classes, with further subdivision into two histologic subtypes within the IDH1-mutant 42 

group. Classification was performed using an XGBoost model with shallow tree depth 43 

(1–3), a 20% held-out test set, and grouped, stratified 5-fold cross-validation to control 44 

for sample-level bias. The machine learning framework distinguished IDH1-mutant 45 

from IDH1–wild-type cells with a ROC–AUC of 0.78 and further discriminated IDH1-46 

mutant astrocytoma from oligodendroglioma cells with a ROC–AUC of 0.81. Feature 47 

importance analysis demonstrated that separation between IDH1-mutant and IDH1– 48

Introduction 74 

Accurate molecular classification of cancer cells is essential for understanding tumor 75 

biology, predicting therapeutic response, and guiding precision medicine 76 

approaches 1,2 . Current methods for molecular characterization typically rely on 77 

genomic, transcriptomic, or immunohistochemical analyses that require cell fixation, 78 

tissue processing, or destructive sample preparation 2–4 .79 

In the context of glioma, contemporary molecular diagnostic approaches, including 80 

DNA methylation profiling and next-generation sequencing (NGS), have transformed 81 

the classification of diffuse gliomas by enabling comprehensive genomic and 82 

epigenetic characterization 5–12 . These advances have refined diagnostic accuracy, 83 

improved prognostic stratification, and facilitated the development of more 84 

personalized treatment strategies. However, despite increasingly sophisticated 85 

molecular classification systems and the widespread use of multimodal therapy 86 

consisting of maximal safe surgical resection followed by radiotherapy and 87 

temozolomide chemotherapy, disease recurrence remains universal 13–15 . This 88 

highlights a critical limitation of current approaches: while genomic and epigenetic 89 

profiling provide valuable information regarding tumor identity, they do not necessarily 90 

capture the dynamic functional states that ultimately govern cellular behavior, 91 

therapeutic response, and disease progression, because they provide only static 92 

snapshots of cellular states and are not readily adaptable to longitudinal monitoring of 93 

living cells. Emerging evidence suggests that metabolic reprogramming, cellular 94 

plasticity, and lineage-specific biological programs contribute substantially to glioma 95 

heterogeneity and treatment resistance 16–19 . Consequently, there is growing interest 96

Coherent Raman spectroscopy offers a unique opportunity to address this challenge. 99 

As a label-free and non-destructive technique, Raman spectroscopy measures 100 

intrinsic molecular vibrations arising from proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, 101

Recent advances in instrumentation, computational analysis, and machine learning 111 

have significantly expanded the utility of Raman spectroscopy in biomedical 112 

applications 27–29 . Raman-based approaches have been successfully used to 113 

distinguish normal and malignant cells, classify tumor subtypes, identify stem-like 114 

populations, monitor drug responses, and characterize metabolic states in cancer 115 

types 24,25,30–39 . Importantly, because Raman spectra capture integrated biochemical 116 

information rather than individual molecular markers, they can reveal functional 117 

cellular phenotypes that may not be apparent from genomic alterations alone. 118

living cells could accurately predict molecular status based solely on intrinsic 137

with computational classification approaches, we sought to identify the spectral 139 

features underlying prediction and to determine the biological processes that 140 

distinguish the analyzed cellular populations. Our findings demonstrate that label-free 141 

Raman spectroscopy captures reproducible biochemical signatures associated with 142 

molecular phenotype and highlight its potential as a non-destructive platform for real-143

Spectral preprocessing 177 

Raw Raman spectra were preprocessed using RADAR (Raman Spectral Analysis 178 

Using Deep Learning for Artifact Removal), a deep-learning framework designed for 179 

simultaneous correction and denoising of Raman spectra. RADAR decomposes each 180 

spectrum into four components corresponding to the baseline, cosmic rays, noise, and 181 

Raman peaks 27,28 . The models were trained using synthetically generated Raman 182 

spectra with known ground-truth components, allowing supervised learning of spectral 183 

artifact removal. 184 

For each acquired spectrum, RADAR estimated the baseline, cosmic-ray, noise, and 185 

peak contributions. The reconstructed peak component was retained for all 186 

subsequent analyses. This procedure removes fluorescence-related baseline 187 

distortions, sporadic cosmic-ray artifacts, and measurement noise in a single 188 

processing step while preserving the spectral features associated with the biochemical 189 

composition of the sample. 190

Identification of cellular regions 194 

Each Raman map contained spectra originating both from cellular material and from 195 

the surrounding medium. Since spectra from the medium contain limited information 196 

about cellular phenotype, an additional filtering step was performed before 197 

classification. 198 

For each Raman map, k-means clustering was applied to partition spectra into groups 199 

with similar spectral characteristics. The resulting clusters were inspected and 200 

assigned either to cellular regions or to background regions dominated by water and 201 

medium signals. Only spectra belonging to cellular regions were retained for further 202 

analysis. 203

Machine-learning model 217 

Classification was performed using the XGBoost framework. For all experiments, the 218 

model consisted of 400 decision trees with a maximum depth of 2 and a binary logistic 219 

objective function. To account for substantial differences in class sizes, class weights 220 

were incorporated during training. The same model configuration was used for all 221 

classification tasks. 222 

Validation procedure 223 

Model performance was evaluated using grouped five-fold cross-validation. All spectra 224 

originating from the same biological sample were assigned to the same fold, ensuring 225 

complete separation between training and test samples. 226 

The classifier generated predictions at the spectrum level. For each sample, a final 227 

class assignment was obtained using majority voting across all spectra belonging to 228 

that sample. Performance metrics were then computed using these sample-level 229 

predictions. 230 

Classification performance was assessed using balanced accuracy, precision, recall, 231 

F1-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC). 232 

Another validation was performed in model co-culture of two cell lines; one cell lines 233 

was labeled with DAPI while the other was cultured unlabeled. The cells were allowed 234

Workflow for Label-Free Prediction of Glioma Cell Identity from Live-Cell Raman 238 

Spectra 239 

The molecular signatures associated with a cellular identity, metabolic or proliferative 240 

status, are encoded within their spontaneous Raman spectra and can be decoded by 241 

machine learning models to enable label-free prediction of cell identity and cellular 242 

state. To determine whether intrinsic biochemical signatures captured by Raman 243 

spectroscopy can be used to predict glioma cell identity, we analyzed a panel of 244 

patient-derived glioma cell lines initially established from surgically resected tumors 245 

(Fig. 1). Cells were initially maintained as neurospheres and subsequently transitioned 246 

to adherent culture conditions to facilitate live-cell Raman imaging. 247 

248

measurements, medium-derived spectra were distinguished from cell-associated 253 

spectra and removed prior to analysis (Fig 1. Panel 3). This preprocessing step 254 

enabled subsequent machine learning models to focus exclusively on intracellular 255 

biochemical fingerprints relevant to cell-type classification. The remaining cellular 256 

spectra were normalized and used for machine learning–based classification. To 257 

isolate intracellular biochemical signals, Raman datasets were processed using our 258

Raman Spectroscopy Reveals IDH1 Mutation–Driven Biochemical 313 

Reprogramming 314 

One of the main objectives of the study was to determine whether Raman 315 

spectroscopy can detect biochemical changes associated with IDH1 status. To 316 

address this question, classifiers were trained to distinguish cells carrying an 317 

engineered IDH1 mutation from their wildtype counterparts. The comparison between 318 

these two isogenic cell lines namely, GBM mutant (GBM mut ) and GBM wildtype 319 

(GBM WT ), achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.81, with ROC AUC and PR AUC values 320 

of 0.86 and 0.98, respectively. These results indicate that the engineered mutation is 321 

associated with reproducible changes in the Raman spectra of live GBM cells. 322

Methodologically, an important aspect of the present workflow is the use of strict 526 

sample-level validation. Although the dataset contains nearly 700,000 spectra, the 527 

effective number of independent observations is determined by the number of 528 

biological samples rather than the number of spectra. To avoid information leakage, 529 

all spectra originating from a given sample were assigned to the same cross-validation 530 

fold and final predictions were generated using sample-level majority voting. This 531 

evaluation protocol provides a more realistic assessment of model performance than 532 

spectrum-level validation. 533 

Another important component of the workflow is the use of RADAR preprocessing 27,28 .534 

Conventional Raman preprocessing pipelines typically rely on a sequence of 535 

independently designed procedures for baseline correction, denoising, and artifact 536 

removal. In contrast, RADAR performs simultaneous decomposition of each spectrum 537 

into baseline, cosmic-ray, noise, and peak components using deep neural networks 538 

trained on synthetic Raman data. By retaining only the reconstructed peak component, 539 

the method removes several sources of spectral variability in a single processing step 540 

while preserving the biochemical information relevant for classification. The use of a 541 

consistent preprocessing framework across all experiments also reduces variability 542 

introduced by manual selection of preprocessing parameters. 543

acquired under controlled experimental conditions. While this setting enables 545 

investigation of specific biological questions, additional validation will be required to 546

identity represent two orthogonal biological axes that shape glioma phenotype. IDH1 564 

mutation primarily influences cellular metabolism, mitochondrial function, and redox 565 

homeostasis, whereas lineage identity determines broader aspects of cellular 566 

architecture, protein composition, lipid organization, and metabolic specialization. 567 

Remarkably, Raman spectroscopy was able to resolve both axes simultaneously using 568 

only intrinsic biochemical information. This capability distinguishes Raman 569 

spectroscopy from conventional molecular assays, which typically interrogate either 570 

genotype or histology independently. 571
