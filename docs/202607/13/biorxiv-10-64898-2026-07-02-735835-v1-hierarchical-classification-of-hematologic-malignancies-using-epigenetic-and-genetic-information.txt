ABSTRACT 94 

Molecular testing in hematology requires different assays for disease subgroup 95 

identification, risk stratification and selection of appropriate treatment regimens. Yet, 96 

molecular tests are not necessarily standardized between diagnostic laboratories, 97 

resulting in varying turnaround times and potentially divergent results. To resolve this 98 

issue and enable single-assay molecular testing, we have developed a hierarchical 99 

classification framework that combines epigenetic and genetic data from whole 100 

genome nanopore sequencing (WGNS) with machine learning to determine disease 101 

entities, epigenetic subgroups (epitypes) and genetic aberrations in hematopoietic 102 

neoplasms. We curated DNA methylation data from 5,420 samples and trained a 103 

classifier allowing entity-level diagnostics featuring 21 conditions, including healthy 104 

controls, acute and chronic myeloid and lymphoid neoplasms. This classifier was 105 

subsequently combined with entity-specific epitype classifiers predicting 44 106 

therapeutically or prognostically relevant states, followed by integration of genetic data. 107 

Benchmarking of the combined (epi-)genetic testing strategy using WGNS confirmed 108 

high accuracy in the detection of diagnostic groups and risk stratification, and identified 109 

diagnosis-defining molecular alterations that were not reported by standard-of-care 110 

work-up. 111

INTRODUCTION 113 

Over the past two decades, molecular diagnostics have enabled improved risk 114 

classification and identification of targetable alterations in hematopoietic neoplasms 1-

115 

> 5

. These discoveries have largely been based on genetic and transcriptomic data, due 116 

to the widespread availability of these technologies across institutes, the development 117 

of standardized workflows, and the capacity to detect genetic driver events. However, 118 

while genetic driver events can be shared across different disease entities, the DNA 119 

methylome intrinsically encodes cell-, lineage-, and disease-specific patterns, thus 120 

potentially enabling a more accurate identification of molecular disease subgroups. In 121 

line with this, DNA methylation analysis has recently been adopted for the molecular 122 

diagnosis of brain cancers and sarcomas, and is now being used increasingly in 123 

molecular tumor boards for solid cancers 6-9 . In hematopoietic neoplasms, DNA 124 

methylation analysis has been used to identify molecular subgroups in various disease 125 

entities such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), acute myeloid leukemia (AML), 126 

chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), 127 

myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), splenic marginal zone lymphoma (SMZL), and 128 

juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia (JMML) 10-20 . However, most DNA methylation-129 

based disease subgroups have not been translated into clinically applicable diagnostic 130 

tools or workflows. We recently sought to address this limitation for JMML by 131 

establishing an international consensus on the definition of DNA methylation 132 

subgroups, so-called epitypes, in this disease, and by developing clinically applicable 133 

tools that enable the faithful classification of individual patients 21 . This effort has 134 

resulted in the first clinical trial that stratifies JMML patients by epitype to receive 135 

different treatments according to their predicted risk (NCT05849662) 22 .136 

More recently, two DNA methylation classifiers spanning different types of acute 137 

leukemia have been developed to identify molecular subgroups, which can be used in 138 

combination with nanopore sequencing 23,24 . These classifiers enable rapid molecular 139 

diagnostics with improved turnaround times compared to the current standard-of-care. 140 

However, these classifiers only allow valid interpretation of prediction results for 141 

patients for which a diagnosis of acute leukemia has been established. Currently, this 142 

requires additional laboratory tests to first establish an entity-level diagnosis, followed 143 

by the selection of an appropriate classifier model. Here, we aimed to overcome this 144 

limitation by enabling combined entity and disease subgroup classification from DNA 145 

methylation data by using a hierarchical classification strategy. First, this strategy 146

DNA methylation reference atlas of hematopoietic neoplasms and healthy cell 155 

types 156 

We collected DNA methylation array data of adult and pediatric hematopoietic 157 

neoplasms and healthy controls, comprising a total of 7,053 samples ( Figure 1A ). A 158 

stringent quality control pipeline was applied to remove probes mapping to sex 159 

chromosomes, non-CpG targets, common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), 160 

as well as previously annotated poor quality probes and probes with low detection p-161 

values 25 . Samples with low-quality methylation array data and duplicated samples that 162 

were included in multiple published datasets were removed, resulting in a final dataset 163 

of 5,420 samples (3,796 hematopoietic neoplasms and 1,624 controls; Supp. Table 164 

1&2 ). This comprised 20 different disease entities, encompassing myeloid and 165 

lymphoid acute and chronic neoplasms (i.e. acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), acute 166 

myeloid leukemia (AML), mixed-phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL), chronic myeloid 167 

leukemia (CML), chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), juvenile myelomonocytic 168 

leukemia (JMML), myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), myelofibrosis (MF), essential 169 

thrombocythemia (ET), polycythemia vera (PV), Noonan-syndrome associated 170 

myeloproliferative disorder (NS-MPD), Burkitt lymphoma (BL), diffuse large B-cell 171 

lymphoma (DLBCL), follicular lymphoma (FL), mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), splenic 172 

marginal zone lymphoma (SMZL), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), B-cell 173 

prolymphocytic leukemia (BPLL) and Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM) patients). 174 

Samples of isolated hematopoietic cell types from blood, bone marrow (BM), tonsils 175 

and spleen (n=578), as well as healthy blood and cord blood samples (n=1,046) were 176 

used as controls ( Figure 1B ). Unsupervised dimension reduction was performed 177

Development of a DNA methylation classifier for hematopoietic neoplasms 219 

In a next step, we aimed to train a DNA methylation classifier that enables prediction 220 

of hematopoietic disease entities based on our reference atlas. After excluding isolated 221 

cell types, the dataset for classifier training consisted of 4,842 samples, comprising 21 222 

classes (20 hematopoietic neoplasms and healthy controls). The data was divided into 223 

a training (n=3,884) and testing (n=958) cohort while ensuring balanced class 224 

distributions ( Figure 2A ). An extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) model was trained 225 

based on the previously determined varCpGs (n=12,627), followed by hyperparameter 226 

tuning and feature selection. The final classifier comprised 932 CpGs and 227 

demonstrated an entity classification accuracy of 95.5% with a median class specificity 228 

of 1 (min: 0.98; max: 1), and sensitivity of 0.93 (min: 0; max: 1; Supp. Figure 2A&B; 229 

Supp. Table 5 ). The per sample class (entity) prediction probabilities ranged from 0.21 230 

to 1.0 (mean: 0.96; median: 1). To determine the prediction probability threshold 231

Hierarchical epigenetic classification of hematopoietic neoplasm disease entity 262 

and epitype 263 

Previously published DNA methylation classifiers required prior laboratory testing to 264 

first determine an entity-level diagnosis before selecting an appropriate epigenetic 265 

subgroup (epitype) classifier. Our newly established classifier addresses this limitation 266 

by directly inferring the respective disease entity for hematopoietic neoplasms from 267 

DNA methylation data. This enables a hierarchical classification strategy in which first 268 

the respective disease entity is predicted, and then, based on the prediction results, 269 

an appropriate epitype classifier is determined and applied to establish a DNA 270 

methylation-based entity and epitype diagnosis ( Figure 3A ). We validated this strategy 271 

for B-ALL, CLL and JMML, as DNA methylation epitypes have been well-established 272 

for these diseases. Validation was conducted in cohorts which had not been included 273 

in classifier training, allowing an unbiased assessment of our classifier in a real-world 274 

scenario. For B-ALL, we used a publicly available dataset 26 and when applying the tier-275 

1 cut-off, 92 out of 133 patients (69.2%) were classified as B-ALL, while 41 out of 133 276 

patients (30.8%) did not reach the prediction cutoff ( Figure 3B; Supp. Table 6 ). 277 

Reanalyzing these patients using the tier-2 threshold, correctly classified 114/133 278 

patients (85.7%) as B-ALL and 19/133 (14.3%) as unknown. Epitypes were 279 

subsequently determined for all patients that were classified as B-ALL according to the 280 

tier-2 threshold. We therefore relied on MARLIN, a recently developed epitype model 281 

for acute leukemias 23 . However, as the originally published neural network-based 282 

MARLIN model was developed for sparse genome-wide sequencing data, we 283 

developed a random-forest based MARLIN model (MARLIN-RF) for improved 284 

prediction accuracy with non-sparse array-based data. Five-fold cross validation 285 

accuracies for MARLIN methylation classes based on this model were 0.96 for AML, 286 

0.95 for B-ALL, 0.94 for T-ALL, 0.95 for MPAL and 0.99 for controls. Using this 287 

MARLIN-RF classifier, 57 B-ALL samples (50%) were classified as high hyperploid 288 

(HeH), 44 (38.6%) as ETV6 :: RUNX1 , 3 (2.6%) as TCF3 :: PBX1/MEF2D -r and 1 (0.9%) 289 

patient as KMT2A -rearranged epitypes ( Figure 3B ). Nine patients (7.9%) did not reach 290

2 threshold, 94 patients (82.5%) received a JMML diagnosis, while 2 patients (1.8%) 310

disease progression for this patient. 324

DISCUSSION 477 

We here describe the development and validation of a novel hierarchical classification 478 

strategy that combines epigenetic and genetic information for molecular work-up in 479 

hematologic malignancies. Our strategy explores a new approach to epigenetic 480 

diagnostics by first establishing an entity level diagnosis which is then followed by 481 

epitype classification choosing appropriate downstream classifiers. This hierarchy of 482 

molecular testing enables the use of a single diagnostic platform on which the pretest 483 

probability for using a certain epitype classifier is established within the same 484 

framework and which does not rely on additional laboratory assays such as flow 485 

cytometry or cytopathology. The backbone of this framework is the currently largest 486 

> (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted July 3, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.02.735835 doi: bioRxiv preprint

collection of DNA methylation data of hematologic malignancies, which we curated as 487 

a reference atlas to train our entity-level classifier. This collection encompasses 21 488 

diseased and healthy states and comprises a comprehensive resource of 489 

hematopoietic neoplasm data sets. As we needed to rely on the, sometimes sparsely, 490 

annotated patients’ diagnoses in publicly available datasets, these annotations might 491 

not necessarily represent the perfect ground truth and probably hinder the 492 

development of a classifier with a 100% accuracy. In addition, diagnostic criteria, e.g. 493 

for AML and MDS, have changed over the last years which might further impact on the 494 

precision in differentiating these entities using the available databases. We tried to 495 

address this issue by training our classifier model on large patient numbers to prevent 496 

overfitting on individuals with misdiagnoses. By validating our classifier in independent 497 

patient cohorts, we were able to prove the success of our strategy by high diagnostic 498 

accuracies and the ability of our classifier to identify patients with likely inaccurate 499 

diagnoses. 500 

We established two prediction cut-offs (tier-1 and -2) for an entity-level diagnosis. 501 

Although these cut-offs reduce the number of patients that can be successfully 502 

assigned to disease entities, they provide an important quality measure to ensure that 503 

predictions reach clinically acceptable accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity values. We 504 

currently recommend to follow tier-1 predictions and use additional clinical and genetic 505 

data for patients where the tier-2 cut-off but not tier-1 is fulfilled. Prediction probabilities 506 

that are below the tier-2 cut-off can be explained by several scenarios: i) samples have 507 

a low tumor-cell content which might cause an intermediate methylation profile 508 

between tumor and normal; ii) the full heterogeneity of disease entities which are only 509 

represented by a low number of patients in our training dataset might not be covered 510 

which results in low prediction probabilities; iii) patients are not treatment naïve which 511 

might impact on classifier performance; iv) patients with overlap syndromes such as 512 

MPN/MDS overlap disorders 32 ; v) disease entities which are not currently included in 513 

our classifier training. It will be a task for future work to generate additional large 514 

datasets for entities which are underrepresented or not represented at all in our 515 

classifier to further improve the performance of our diagnostic classifier model. 516 

We validated the functionality of our classification strategy across different 517 

technological platforms, namely DNA methylation arrays and nanopore-sequencing. In 518 

combination with our classifier, the latter technology allows for rapid diagnostic 519 

work-ups as an entity level diagnosis can already be established within hours of 520

sequencing. A similar strategy has recently been clinically validated for rapid work-up 521 

of acute leukemias and CNS tumors, including intra-operative nanopore-sequencing 522 

for surgical decision making 23,28 . Importantly, combining DNA methylation classifiers 523 

with WGNS enables combined epigenetic and genetic work-up. The latter is currently 524 

required for establishment of an accurate diagnosis for many hematologic 525 

malignancies according to WHO definitions and key for identification of suitable 526 

targeted therapies and risk group assignment 2,3,5 . Here, we benchmarked for the first 527 

time this combined work-up for hematologic neoplasms in a cohort of AML patients and 528 

proved non-inferiority of our single platform hierarchical WGNS testing strategy in 529 

assigning WHO diagnoses and ELN2022 risk groups compared to SOC. Additionally, 530 

we identified a diagnosis-defining NUP98 -rearrangement that was missed by SOC 531 

work-up, thus underscoring the power of long-read WGNS for detection of SVs. Yet, a 532 

limitation of our WGNS approach was the low sensitivity for the detection of SNVs with 533 

low VAFs which is likely caused by differences in sequencing coverage between panel-534 

sequencing and WGNS. However, this limitation might be overcome by future studies 535 

that combine whole genome read-outs with adaptive sampling or CRIPSR/Cas9 536 

enrichments for an improved coverage at genomic loci with recurrent mutations in 537 

hematologic malignancies. 538 

The integrative profiling of genetic and epigenetic markers will likely gain additionally 539 

significance in the next decades as an increasing number of molecular alterations are 540 

discovered that serve as biomarkers for targeted therapies 33 . Rapid profiling of these 541 

alterations will become a central part of the initial diagnostic work-up and will likely be 542 

subject of future clinical trials. We demonstrate that our newly developed hierarchical 543 

testing strategy can serve as a versatile framework for single platform diagnostic 544 

work-up which can be easily amended with additional epitype classifiers that might be 545 

developed in the future. 546

Quality control and processing of DNA methylation array data 569 

Quality control of DNA methylation array data was conducted using adapted versions 570 

of conumee2 (https://github.com/mwsill/conumee2) and minfi 571 

(https://github.com/mwsill/minfi) that were optimized for cross platform DNA 572 

methylation array analysis including EPICv2 36,37 . First, the average of the log2 median 573 

of methylated and unmethylated channels per sample was calculated and samples 574 

with a value of <10.5 removed. Second, we calculated the array noise per sample using 575 

the conummee2 “CNV.fit” function and removed samples with a value of >0.5. DNA 576 

methylation array processing was performed using minfi 577 

(https://github.com/mwsill/minfi) 36 . We first applied a probe-level filtering and removed 578 

probes with detection p-values ≥0.01, masked probes across any DNA methylation 579 

array version, probes mapping to sex chromosomes or common SNPs and non-CpG 580 

targeting probes. Probe annotations, masking and filtering criteria were derived from 581

https://zwdzwd.github.io/InfiniumAnnotation 25 . We furthermore included only CpG sites 582 

that were covered across all Illumina DNA methylation array version (450k, EPICv1 583 

and EPICv2). DNA methylation beta values were calculated from probe intensity levels 584 

and probes with “NA” values in any sample removed. DNA methylation array data was 585 

normalized using beta-mixture quantile (BMIQ) normalization as implemented in the 586 

watermelon (version 2.12.0) R package 38,39 . As patient samples might be duplicated in 587 

publicly available datasets, we additionally applied a correlation filter to remove closely 588 

identical DNA methylation arrays. For this, we calculated sample-wise correlation 589 

values across the 5000 most variable CpG probes based on standard deviation. 590 

Samples were considered duplicated if Pearson’s correlation values exceeded 0.999, 591 

and only one of the affected samples was retained in the dataset. 592

DNA methylation classifier training 618 

For classifier training, we focused on the previously determined 12,627 CpG sites with 619 

a standard deviation of more than 0.25 across all samples. Data from healthy cell types 620 

were removed from the dataset. The data was then split into a training (n=3,884) and 621 

testing (n=958) cohort using the caret (version 7.0-1) “createDataPartition” function 622 

with a training fraction of 0.8 43 . An Extreme Gradient Boosting Model (XGBoost) was 623 

trained for multi-class classification using the caret "xgbTree" method 44 .624 

Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using a 5-fold cross validations strategy 625 

followed by model training using the following settings: nround=270, eta=0.2, 626 

max_depth=1, gamma=0.5, colsample_bytree=0.4, min_child_weight=2 and 627 

subsample=0.5. Feature selection was performed by applying the caret “varImp” 628 

function and CpG sites with a feature importance of >0.1 selected (n=932). A final 629 

model was trained based on the previously determined hyperparameters and selected 630 

CpG sites. ROC curves were calculated using the pROC (version 1.19.0.1) R package 631 

based on binarized predictions (concordance with annotated diagnosis) and maximum 632

CLL 729 

A CLL epitype classifier was developed based on a previously published dataset and 730 

annotated epitypes 16 . Data was therefore randomly split into a training (n=121) and a 731 

testing (n=38) cohort using the caret “createDataPartition” function and 1,048 CpGs 732 

selected that overlapped with a previously determined set of probes localized at 733 

transcription factor (TF) binding sites of six CLL relevant TFs 16,43 . An XGBoost classifier 734

max_depth=6, gamma=0, colsample_bytree=1, min_child_weight=1 and 736

classification with high specificity and sensitivity 45 . Thereby, a CLL epitype threshold of 740 

0.78 was determined. For analysis of WGNS data, the classifier was adjusted by 741 

training based on binarized DNA methylation data with a beta-value cutoff 0.6. 742
